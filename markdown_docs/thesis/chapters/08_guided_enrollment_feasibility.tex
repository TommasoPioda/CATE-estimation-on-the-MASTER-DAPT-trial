\section{The Premise.}
\label{sec:ch8-premise}

Chapter 6 leaves two facts standing together. Five independent estimators
recover the
trial's average effect, and none of them finds a per-patient dispersion of usable magnitude. 
Its closing section also gives a
mechanism for the second fact: MASTER DAPT enrolls only patients already flagged at high
bleeding risk, so the bleeding-risk axis is compressed before any model sees a single
patient, and the aggregate ischemic score sums a MI effect and a stroke effect of
opposite sign, so a real cancellation looks identical to a real absence. The same
compression is why bleeding, with 506 events, is predicted worse (AUROC 0.62) than
cardiovascular death, with 81 (AUROC 0.76) in Chapter 5: enrollment flattened precisely
the dimension a bleeding model would need.

\vspace{10pt}

This weakens the premise of the strategy Chapter 3 was built for. An enrollment
mechanism is designed to prioritize patients using an estimated joint bleeding--ischemic
score; if the conflict between the two axes is mostly compression and cancellation rather
than a reproducible signal, that score may contain little information for selection. The question
this chapter asks is not whether guided enrollment would help MASTER DAPT, but whether
the machinery of Chapter 3, applied with the same criteria, can still be distinguished
from a machinery that selects at random. The estimated CATE is taken at face value,
cohorts are built from it, and what is measured is whether those cohorts differ in
\textbf{observed} bleeding and ischemic events.

\paragraph{Selection policies.}
As previously anticipated, the trade-off plane is built from two independent components:
the bleeding CATE and the weighted ischemic CATE.
This multidimensionality allows for several ways to combine them into a single scalar score.
The six non-random policy variants and the random control are explained below.

Write $b$ for bleeding benefit of shortening and $i$ for the weighted ischemic
benefit, with positive values favouring shortening on both axes. The policy names and
formulas are fixed as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{\textbf{Net-benefit / win-win}} is the scalar sum
          $s_{\mathrm{NB}}=b+i$. It rewards simultaneous improvement in bleeding and
          ischemic outcomes and points toward the top-right quadrant.

    \item \emph{\textbf{Conflict / trade-off}} is the scalar difference
          $s_{\mathrm{conflict}}=b-i$. It increases when bleeding benefit and ischemic
          benefit disagree and points toward the bottom-right direction.

    \item \emph{\textbf{Angle net-benefit}} uses median-centred, IQR-rescaled
          coordinates $(x,y)$ and targets $+45^\circ$, with centred Q1 as its preferred
          tier.

    \item \emph{\textbf{Angle conflict}} applies $y\mapsto-y$ before quadrant,
          magnitude and angular-distance calculations; this is the $-45^\circ$ direction
          on the original centred plane and makes original Q4 its preferred tier.

    \item The two \emph{\textbf{pure ischemic}} controls rank $i$ and $-i$.

    \item The \emph{\textbf{random policy}} ignores estimated CATEs and defines the
          no-guidance control.
\end{itemize}

These scores are exploratory modelling constructs, not clinically validated utilities.
In particular, the relative weighting of death, MI and stroke is fixed for this analysis
rather than elicited from patients, clinicians or a prespecified decision framework.
A change in an observed cohort event rate therefore describes the association induced by
the selection rule; it does not, by itself, demonstrate a causal treatment benefit or a
clinically preferable enrollment policy.

\paragraph{Saved-result convention.}
The generated artifacts carry an explicit score-convention tag. Every displayed
``Conflict'' row therefore denotes $b-i$, every ``Net-benefit'' row denotes $b+i$, and
the two Angle rules are evaluated separately at $+45^\circ$ and $-45^\circ$.

Throughout this chapter, the selection scores use the aggregation of the three
ischemic endpoints weighted by the same ISCH\_WEIGHTS used in Chapter 6. The observed
ischemic outcome reported for every mechanism is the proportion of patients with at
least one of death, MI or stroke, with each patient counted once. This matches the saved
per-round counts and figures of Mechanism~1 and is used uniformly in the tables for
Mechanisms~2--5. The bleeding outcome is the same as in Chapter 6 throughout.

\vspace{10pt}

The Angle scores use median-centred, IQR-rescaled plane coordinates rather than a
linear combination, so they are not scalar sums/differences in the same sense as the two
composite policies.
All mechanisms use 100 seeded replications, paired across policies by run identifier.
Every displayed selected or included cohort excludes the common random 1,000-patient seed,
so comparisons across mechanisms are not diluted by patients no policy was able to choose.
For Mechanisms~4--5, each replication draws a new seed cohort and arrival order and
performs its own hyperparameter tuning on that seed cohort.

\paragraph{The Starting point.}
All the mechanisms in this chapter start from 1,000 patients drawn at random from the
full cohort of 4,579 patients. In every replication, the forest hyperparameters are
optimized on that replication's seed cohort over 20 Optuna trials, using the same
cross-validated AUTOC lower-bound criterion on the bleeding endpoint used throughout
this chapter. The resulting configuration is then held fixed for the rest of that run.

\paragraph{The Mirror Test}
The two single-axis controls exist to answer a question the diagonal rule and the two
composite scores cannot: if the ischemic axis genuinely ranks patients by ischemic
vulnerability, pushing it in one direction should symmetrically move how many real
ischemic-event patients are enrolled early versus deferred. The idea is to prove that if
the ischemic axis is used to select patients with fewer ischemic events, if the axis is reversed,
it should select patients with more ischemic events.

\section{Mechanism 1 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
The enrollment mechanism follows the same skeleton as Chapter 3. It differs from every
mechanism introduced there in how a round is decided: rather than sampling a batch and
keeping its single best candidate, every not-yet-enrolled patient is scored with the
current model, an annealed Gumbel perturbation of that ranking selects 100 patients,
the model is refit on the enlarged cohort, and the round repeats. Enrollment stops at
3,500 of the 4,579 patients. The temperature decreases as the cohort grows, shifting
selection from exploration toward exploitation as described in Chapter 3. In the reported
runs, $\tau(n_{\mathrm{tot}})=5\exp(-8n_{\mathrm{tot}}/4{,}579)$ and the uncertainty bonus is
zero: this is stochastic exploration of the score ranking, not selection by model
uncertainty.

\paragraph{Policies.}
The mechanism is evaluated using the six non-random policies, with random enrollment
used as a control. The temperature is progressively decreased as the cohort grows, 
allowing greater exploration during the early rounds and increasingly deterministic 
selection in later rounds.

\section{Mechanism 2 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
Mechanism 2 keeps the same seed-and-refit skeleton as described in
Chapter 3. At every round, 100 patients are drawn uniformly at random from the
not-yet-enrolled pool and ranked by the current model's score; the better half is enrolled
with the treatment and outcome they actually received, and the worse half is permanently
discarded rather than returned to the pool. The model is refit after every round.
Enrollment continues until the 3,579-patient pool is exhausted, at 36 rounds: the last
round samples only the 79 patients left, so the final split is 1,789 enrolled against
1,790 discarded, for a final cohort of 2,789 patients, the same size Mechanism 3 and the
bandit mechanisms reach.

\paragraph{Selection rules.}
All the policies introduced earlier in this chapter rank the random sample here.
Each rule is run over 100 run-specific seed cohorts paired across policies by run
identifier; the model is tuned and refit separately in each replication.

\section{Mechanism 3 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
Mechanism 3 keeps the same seed-and-refit skeleton as described in Chapter 3.
The remaining 3,579 patients arrive as 1,789 pairs,
with a single unpaired patient left over at the end of the stream and excluded from both
groups below. Each pair is resolved by a duel rule, the winner is enrolled with the
treatment and outcome they actually received, and the loser is permanently excluded
rather than returned to the pool for a later round. The model is refit every 100
enrollments.

\vspace{10pt}

The duel rule is occasionally overridden by the decaying-probability schedule defined in
Chapter 3 for Mechanism 3: with probability $p(n) = \max(e^{-n/300}, 0.05)$, where $n$
counts patients enrolled past the seed, the pair is resolved instead by keeping whichever
of the two candidates carries the larger model uncertainty. Enrollment stops once the
arriving stream is exhausted, at 2,789 of the 4,579 patients: the 1,000-patient seed plus
1,789 duel winners, against 1,789 duel losers left permanently outside the cohort.

\paragraph{Duel rules.}
Every non-random policy introduced earlier in this chapter reduces naturally to a
pairwise comparison and is run here as a duel rule, together with Random as a control:
Net-benefit, Conflict, Angle conflict, Angle net-benefit, $-$isch and $+$isch.
Angle conflict and Angle net-benefit are distinct duel rules: the former reflects the
centred ischemic coordinate and targets $-45^\circ$; the latter keeps the original
coordinate and targets $+45^\circ$. Each duel rule is run over 100 run-specific seed
cohorts paired across policies by run identifier; the model is tuned and refit separately
in each replication to average out the noise of a single arrival order.

\section{Mechanisms 4 and 5 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
Mechanisms 4 and 5 replace Mechanism 3's decaying-probability override with two
uncertainty-aware selection rules: the UCB-style and Thompson-style rules,
introduced in Chapter 3. Patients still
arrive and duel two at a time, the winner enrolled with the treatment and outcome they
actually received and the loser discarded, and the model is refit every 100 enrollments.
The UCB-style heuristic keeps the candidate with the higher score-plus-uncertainty-bonus,
a rule with no random component. It is inspired by UCB's
optimism-under-uncertainty principle, rather than an implementation of classical UCB1.
The Thompson-style rule instead draws one sample per candidate from a normal
distribution centred on the model's score, with the model's own uncertainty as the scale,
and keeps the higher draw. A coin-flip random baseline, drawn from the same arriving
pairs, grows alongside the guided cohort so both are compared at a common $n$; enrollment
reaches the same final cohort size of 2,789 patients as Mechanism 3.

\paragraph{Selection rules.}
Both uncertainty-aware rules are run under the two linear scores introduced earlier in
this chapter, net-benefit and conflict, for four policy--regime combinations. The 100
replications use run-specific seed cohorts and arrival orders. Within each replication,
the four combinations share the same seed cohort and stream. Each post-seed selected
cohort is compared both with the rejected loser from the same duels and with an
independent coin-flip baseline drawn from the same arriving pairs. The common
1,000-patient seed is excluded from all reported groups. The UCB-style heuristic is
deterministic conditional on a given cohort and arrival order, whereas the Thompson-style
rule adds its own run-specific sampling variation.

The internal comparison in Table~\ref{tab:ch8-bandit-internal-rate-comparison} asks whether
the rule sorts the patients it sees in the intended direction. It does not do so for the
UCB-style heuristic: selected patients have higher any-ischaemic-event rates than their
rejected duel partners under both conflict ($+0.20$ pp, $p=0.014$) and net-benefit
($+0.36$ pp, $p<0.001$). Neither UCB bleeding contrast is significant. Under Thompson
sampling, conflict yields a lower selected rate of $-0.12$ pp ($p=0.132$) and net-benefit
a difference of $+0.02$ pp ($p=0.799$), neither distinguishable from zero; its bleeding
contrasts are also non-significant. Thus none of the Mechanism~4--5 internal comparisons
shows a significant reduction in any-ischaemic events among selected rather than rejected
patients.

On the any-ischaemic-event endpoint, the UCB-style heuristic produces a small increase
under conflict ($+0.08$ pp, $p=0.056$) and a larger increase under net-benefit
($+0.16$ pp, $p=0.004$). Thompson sampling produces a small decrease under conflict

\vspace{10pt}

Although the UCB-style heuristic has no internal random component, its selected cohort
varies across replications because both the initial cohort and the arrival order are
redrawn. The Thompson-style rule adds sampling variation on top of those two sources. In
each replication, selected and rejected are the complementary winner and loser of every
duel; the Random baseline is an independent coin-flip choice from those same pairs. All
reported contrasts are paired by replication, so the SEM summarizes run-to-run variation
of the corresponding paired difference.

\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter reports two distinct paired contrasts: selected versus rejected patients
measures sorting within a rule, whereas selected versus Random tests whether guidance
changes the post-seed cohort relative to unguided enrollment. Using the direct Random
contrast, Conflict lowers the any-ischaemic-event rate under Mechanism~1 ($-0.16$ pp,
$p<0.001$) and Mechanism~2 ($-0.17$ pp, $p=0.010$), but the direction does not carry
consistently into the pairwise mechanisms. The difference is $+0.03$ pp ($p=0.335$) under
Mechanism~3, $+0.08$ pp ($p=0.056$) under the UCB-style heuristic, and $-0.06$ pp
($p=0.139$) under the Thompson-style rule. The larger internal separations for
Mechanisms~1 and 2 ($-0.51$ and $-0.32$ pp) answer the different question of how strongly
each rule splits the patients it evaluates. The Mechanism~4--5 internal contrasts give no
additional evidence in the desired direction: UCB selects more any-ischaemic events than
its rejected duel partners ($+0.20$ pp under conflict and $+0.36$ pp under net-benefit),
while neither Thompson contrast is significant.

\vspace{10pt}

The mirror test provides the clearest evidence that the weighted ischemic score is
associated with the observed any-ischaemic-event outcome. Under Mechanism~2, the
included-versus-discarded contrast reverses from $+0.38$ pp for $+$isch ($p<0.001$) to
$-0.40$ pp for $-$isch ($p=0.013$). Against Random directly, the corresponding
differences remain significant but shrink to $+0.18$ pp ($p=0.005$) and $-0.21$ pp
($p=0.014$). The axis estimated by the Chapter~6 causal forests therefore carries a
selection signal, even though the score remains a weighted CATE while the reported
outcome is the observed union of death, MI and stroke.

\vspace{10pt}

The bleeding axis also moves inconsistently. Against Random, Angle net-benefit raises the
bleeding rate under Mechanisms~1 and 2 ($+0.17$ pp, $p=0.004$, and $+0.16$ pp,
$p=0.039$). The $+$isch rule raises it under Mechanisms~1, 2 and 3 ($+0.17$, $+0.18$ and
$+0.19$ pp; all $p\leq0.047$). Conflict does not significantly change bleeding against
Random under Mechanisms~1--3, and none of the four Mechanism~4--5 bleeding differences is
significant; their absolute magnitudes are at most $0.05$ pp and all $p$-values are at
least $0.470$.

\vspace{10pt}

Three policy--mechanism combinations move both axes significantly in the same direct
comparison with Random. Under Mechanism~1, Angle conflict lowers bleeding by $0.13$ pp and
ischemic events by $0.20$ pp (both $p=0.003$ or smaller). Under Mechanism~2, $+$isch
raises them by $0.18$ pp ($p=0.047$) and $0.18$ pp ($p=0.005$); under Mechanism~3 it raises
them by $0.19$ pp ($p=0.014$) and $0.19$ pp ($p<0.001$). Neither Mechanism~4 nor
Mechanism~5 moves both axes significantly. These directions describe cohort composition,
not a clinical utility ordering.

\vspace{10pt}

The chapter's opening question was whether the enrollment machinery of Chapter~3 remains
distinguishable from random selection once its premise, a real trade-off between two
well-separated risk axes, is weakened. Several policies produce small but reproducible
direct differences from Random after the common seed is removed. Internal
selected-versus-rejected separations are often larger, as expected, but cannot be
interpreted as policy-versus-Random effects. The compression described in Chapter~6
narrows what a selection rule can find without eliminating it. None of these changes in
observed cohort composition validates the CATE ranking as causal targeting, establishes a
clinical benefit, or demonstrates improved trial precision at a fixed sample size.
