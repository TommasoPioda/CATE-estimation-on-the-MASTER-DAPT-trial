\section{The Premise Removed by Chapter 6}

Chapter 6 leaves two facts standing together. Five independent estimators
recover the
trial's average effect, and none of them finds a per-patient dispersion of usable magnitude. 
Its closing section also gives a
mechanism for the second fact: MASTER DAPT enrolls only patients already flagged at high
bleeding risk, so the bleeding-risk axis is compressed before any model sees a single
patient, and the aggregate ischemic score sums a MI effect and a stroke effect of
opposite sign, so a real cancellation looks identical to a real absence. The same
compression is why bleeding, with 506 events, is predicted worse (AUROC 0.62) than
cardiovascular death, with 81 (AUROC 0.76) in Chapter 5: enrollment flattened precisely
the dimension a bleeding model would need.

\vspace{10pt}

This removes the premise of the strategy Chapter 3 was built for. An enrollment
mechanism aims to select the cohort with the best combined gain in bleeding and ischemic
risk; if the conflict between the two axes is mostly compression and cancellation rather
than a real signal, there is nothing left for the mechanism to select on. The question
this chapter asks is not whether guided enrollment would help MASTER DAPT, but whether
the machinery of Chapter 3, applied with the same criteria, can still be distinguished
from a machinery that selects at random. The estimated CATE is taken at face value,
cohorts are built from it, and what is measured is whether those cohorts differ in
\textbf{observed} bleeding and ischemic events.

\paragraph{Five policies.}
As previously anticipated, the trade-off plane is built from two indipendent components,
The bleeding CATE and the weighted ischemic CATE.
This multidimensionality allows for several ways to combine them into a single scalar score.
The five policies and the control are explained below.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The \emph{\textbf{win-win score}} combines the two components, prioritising patients in the top-right quadrant, where both bleeding and ischemic outcomes are expected to improve.
    
    \item The \emph{\textbf{trade-off score}} subtracts the ischemic component from the bleeding component, prioritising patients in the bottom-right quadrant, where a reduction in bleeding risk is accompanied by an increase in ischemic risk.
    
    \item The \emph{\textbf{diagonal scores}} uses the geometry of the treatment-effect plane directly. Patients in the win-win quadrant are ranked above those outside it; within each quadrant, ties are resolved according to distance from the origin or proximity to the $+45^\circ$ diagonal.
    
    \item The \emph{\textbf{pure ischemic score}} rank patients according to the ischemic component alone, using the opposite direction as control.
    
    \item The \emph{\textbf{random policy}} enrolls patients without using their estimated CATEs, this policy is our "0" value.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{10pt}

The diagonal-score uses raw coordinates rather than a linear combination
to rank the patients, so it is not a scalar score in the same sense as the others.
Each policy is run over 100 independent seed cohorts, under two batch-sampling
temperatures: one that occasionally accepts a candidate other than the top-scoring one
within a batch, following the exploration rule of Chapter 3's Mechanism 1.

\paragraph{The Starting point.}
All the mechanisms in this chapter start from the same amount of 1.000 patients drawn 
at random from the full cohort of 4,579-patients. Once the seed is sampled the 
forest is tuned 20 times on the seed cohort, by the same cross-validated AUTOC lower-bound criterion
on the bleeding endpoint used throughout this chapter; the tuned configuration is then
kept fixed for the rest of the run.

\section{The Mirror Test}
The two single-axis controls exist to answer a question the diagonal rule and the two
composite scores cannot: if the ischemic axis genuinely ranks patients by ischemic
vulnerability, pushing it in one direction should symmetrically move how many real
ischemic-event patients are enrolled early versus deferred. The idea is to prove that if
the ischemic axis is used to select patients with fewer ischemic events, if the axis is reversed,
it should select patients with more ischemic events.

\section{Mechanism 1 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
The enrollment mechanism follows the same skeleton as Chapter 3. It differs from every
mechanism introduced there in how a round is decided: rather than sampling a batch and
keeping its single best candidate, every not-yet-enrolled patient is scored with the
current model, the 100 highest-scoring are enrolled, the model is refit on the enlarged
cohort, and the round repeats. Enrollment stops at 4,200 of the 4,579 patients.
To avoid a completely greedy selection, the ranking is exploited 
through an annealed selection strategy that progressively shifts from exploration 
to exploitation decribed in Chapter 3. 

\paragraph{Policies.}
The mechanism is evaluated using the five non-random policies, with random enrollment 
used as a control. The temperature is progressively decreased as the cohort grows, 
allowing greater exploration during the early rounds and increasingly deterministic 
selection in later rounds.

\section{Mechanism 2 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
Mechanism 2 keeps the same seed-and-refit skeleton as described in
Chapter 3. At every round, 100 patients are drawn uniformly at random from the
not-yet-enrolled pool and ranked by the current model's score; the better half is enrolled
with the treatment and outcome they actually received, and the worse half is permanently
discarded rather than returned to the pool. The model is refit after every round.
Enrollment continues until the 3,579-patient pool is exhausted, at 36 rounds: the last
round samples only the 79 patients left, so the final split is 1,789 enrolled against
1,790 discarded, for a final cohort of 2,789 patients, the same size Mechanism 3 and the
bandit mechanisms reach.

\paragraph{Selection rules.}
All the policies introduced earlier in this chapter rank the random sample here. 
Each rule is run over 100 independent seed cohorts, each tuned and refit independently.

Table~\ref{tab:ch8-mechanism2-rate-comparison} reports the difference in observed
event rates between included and discarded patients, together with the combined
standard error of the two groups. On the ischaemic axis, the trade-off score
shows the largest separation, with a $0.53$ pp lower event rate among included
patients. The win-win score also produces a clear separation, with a difference
of $0.34$ pp, while the diagonal score shows the smallest difference, at
$0.21$ pp. In all three cases, the observed ischaemic event rate is lower among
included patients.

The pattern is different on the bleeding axis. The trade-off score again
favours the included group, which has a $0.31$ pp lower bleeding rate. The
win-win and diagonal scores instead produce slightly higher bleeding rates
among included patients, by $0.15$ pp and $0.35$ pp, respectively.

Overall, the trade-off score provides the clearest separation on both axes,
whereas the win-win score mainly separates patients along the ischaemic axis.
The diagonal score produces the weakest separation. This ordering is
consistent with the pattern observed for Mechanism~1.

\section{Mechanism 3 on the Trade-off Plane}

\paragraph{The mechanism.}
Mechanism 3 keeps the same seed-and-refit skeleton as described in Chapter 3.
The remaining 3,579 patients arrive as 1,789 pairs,
with a single unpaired patient left over at the end of the stream and excluded from both
groups below. Each pair is resolved by a duel rule, the winner is enrolled with the
treatment and outcome they actually received, and the loser is permanently excluded
rather than returned to the pool for a later round. The model is refit every 100
enrollments.

\vspace{10pt}

As in Mechanism 1, a decaying probability occasionally overrides the duel rule: with
probability $p(n) = \max(e^{-n/300}, 0.05)$, where $n$ counts patients enrolled past the
seed, the pair is resolved instead by keeping whichever of the two candidates carries the
larger model uncertainty. Enrollment stops once the arriving stream is exhausted, at
2,789 of the 4,579 patients: the 1,000-patient seed plus 1,789 duel winners, against
1,789 duel losers left permanently outside the cohort.

\paragraph{Duel rules.}
Two of the five policies introduced earlier in this chapter reduce naturally to a
pairwise comparison and are run as duel rules here. The \emph{diagonal score} keeps,
between two patients who both fall in the win-win quadrant, the one further from the
origin; between two who both fall in the opposite quadrant, the one closer to it; and
between any other pair, the one closer to the $+45^\circ$ diagonal. The \emph{trade-off
score} keeps outright the patient with the larger bleeding-minus-ischemic difference.
Each rule is run over 50 independent seed cohorts, each tuned and refit independently, to
average out the noise of a single arrival order.

\section{Mechanisms 4 and 5 on the Trade-off Plane}

Mechanisms 4 and 5 of Chapter 3, UCB1 and Thompson sampling, are also run on this cohort,
over a duel of arriving pairs rather than a whole-pool sweep, with the exploration bonus
active.

Both bandits reach a real, positive gap over random, about a third of the ceiling
reachable on the identical arrivals if the referee itself had been doing the choosing.
The gap is not the bottleneck's whole story: UCB1 is deterministic across all 30 replicate
runs, since its exploration bonus is a fixed function of the current model's uncertainty
with no random component, while Thompson's sampling step gives it run-to-run variation.
