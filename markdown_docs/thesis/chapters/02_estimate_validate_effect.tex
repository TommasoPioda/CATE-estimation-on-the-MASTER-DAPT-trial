\section{Problem Description: Estimating, Validating and exploiting Heterogeneous Treatment Effects}

A randomized clinical trial is a study in which patients are randomly assigned to one treatment, trying to estimate one number:
the average treatment effect across all patients. This is the standard approach to clinical trials, and serves the
purpose of estimating whether a treatment is effective. This measure of effectiveness does not say anything specific
about individual patients. The fact that a treatment is effective on average does not guarantee its effectiveness
on every patient. Some patients may benefit from the treatment while others may be harmed by it.

The object of this thesis is to estimate the heterogeneity of treatment effects in a randomized clinical trial,
and to use it to reduce the number of patients included in the trial while maintaining the same statistical power,
maximizing the benefit of the treatment for the patients who are included.
This thesis aims to be different from a standard clinical trial, building the foundations for the next generation
of clinical trials, which are more efficient and ethical by including only the patients most likely to benefit
from the experimental treatment.

\section{What Is Estimated, and Why It Is Not Validated Like a Prediction}


To decide which patients should be included in the trial, we would ideally need a measure 
of the treatment effect for each individual patient. This quantity is usually referred to
as the individual treatment effect (ITE): the difference between the outcome that a patient 
would experience under treatment $T=1$ and the outcome that the same 
patient would experience under treatment $T=0$.

However, the ITE cannot be observed directly. For each patient, only one of the two 
potential outcomes is observed, because each patient receives exactly one treatment. 
The outcome that would have occurred under the treatment not received is therefore unobserved. 
This is the fundamental problem of causal inference, and it cannot be solved simply by increasing the sample size.

We therefore consider the conditional average treatment effect (CATE),

\[
\boxed{
\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid X=x]
}
\]

which represents the average treatment effect among patients with characteristics $X=x$. 
The CATE provides an estimable population-level counterpart to the unobservable ITE: 
rather than estimating the exact treatment effect for a single patient, it estimates the
expected treatment effect for patients with similar observed characteristics.

A direct consequence of this is that CATE estimates cannot be validated by 
comparing them with an observed ground truth at the individual level. There is no 
outcome that can be used as the ``correct'' treatment effect for a given patient, because
one of the two potential outcomes is always missing. Validation must therefore be 
performed at the aggregate level, by evaluating whether groups of patients with different 
estimated treatment effects actually exhibit different treatment effects in the randomized 
data. Within such groups, the treatment effect can be estimated by comparing the outcomes 
of patients assigned to the different treatment arms.

This constraint is fundamental to the analysis: every validation strategy used in this 
thesis follows from the fact that treatment effects are not directly observable at the 
individual level. The objective is therefore not to verify whether the estimated CATE 
is exactly correct for each patient, but whether the heterogeneity identified by the 
model is supported by the randomized data and can be reliably exploited for patient selection.

\section{Five Families of Estimators, Chosen to act Differently}
\label{sec:five-estimators}

No single method is used, because a result that depends on the internal
assumptions of one algorithm is not distinguishable from an artefact of that
algorithm. Five estimator families are used instead, deliberately spanning
different ways of failing, so that a conclusion which survives all five stops
being attributable to any one of them.

\paragraph{Two-model meta-learner (T-learner).}
The simplest model of the five \citep{salditt2024}: it fits one outcome model per treatment arm,
$\hat\mu_1(x)$ and $\hat\mu_0(x)$, and take their difference as

\[
\hat\tau(x) = \hat\mu_1(x) - \hat\mu_0(x),
\]

Its strength is that any
regression or classification method can be reused as a component; its weakness
is the same fact seen from the other side — it inherits every bias of the two
component models, and small independent errors in each one do not cancel in
the subtraction, they add. Two consequences follow for how $\hat\mu_1$ and $\hat\mu_0$ are built. Both
arms need the same model family, tuned separately on each arm's data:
differencing two algorithms with different inductive biases, a random forest
against a logistic regression for instance, produces a surface whose variation
reflects those biases as much as any treatment effect, and at the level of the
output that spurious variation is indistinguishable from heterogeneity. Both
arms' outcome probabilities also need to be calibrated. Calibration is a
monotone transformation, so it leaves each model's discrimination and its
ranking of patients unchanged, leaving classification performance unaffected.
A difference is affected: a distortion common to both arms cancels out of a
ranking metric such as AUROC but not out of a subtraction, so two
uncalibrated, well-discriminating models can still produce an apparent effect
where the true probabilities agree.

\paragraph{Causal forest.}
The causal forest estimates the treatment effect $\tau(x)$ directly,
rather than fitting two separate outcome models and taking their
difference. Its objective is to partition the
covariate space into groups of patients for whom the treatment effect is expected to differ,
and then estimate a local treatment effect within each region.
It uses double machine learning \citep{kennedy2023} and the 
causal-forest procedure of \citet{wager2018} and \citet{athey2019grf}:
writing $\hat m(x) = \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid X=x]$ and
$\hat e(x) = \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[T \mid X=x]$ for the fitted outcome and
treatment models, the forest works on the residuals
\[
\tilde Y_i = Y_i - \hat m(X_i), \qquad \tilde T_i = T_i - \hat e(X_i),
\]
and within each leaf estimates $\tau(x)$ as the coefficient of the local
regression of $\tilde Y_i$ on $\tilde T_i$.

This step corrects for confounding when treatment assignment depends on
$X$. Under randomization the propensity $\hat e(x)$ is constant across
patients by design, so there is no confounding bias to remove; the
residualization of $Y$ still lowers the variance of $\hat\tau(x)$, in the
same way covariate adjustment improves precision in a randomized trial, by
removing prognostic variation unrelated to treatment. The forest also uses
\emph{honest} splitting: one subset of the data is used to determine the
tree structure, while a separate subset is used to estimate the treatment
effects within each leaf. Honest splitting addresses a different problem,
overfitting from using the same observations to both choose a split and
estimate the effect inside it, and it remains relevant whether or not
treatment is randomized.

\paragraph{Causal forest.}

The causal forest estimates treatment-effect heterogeneity directly, rather than fitting two
separate outcome models and subtracting their predictions. Its objective is to partition the
covariate space into groups of patients for whom the treatment effect is expected to differ,
and then estimate a local treatment effect within each region.

The implementation used here combines the causal-forest framework of
\citet{wager2018} and \citet{athey2019grf} with double machine learning
\citep{kennedy2023}. Before the forest searches for heterogeneity, it removes the parts
of the outcome and treatment assignment that can already be explained by the observed
covariates. Let

\[
\hat m(x) = \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[Y \mid X=x]
\]

denote the expected outcome given the covariates, and

\[
\hat e(x) = \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[T \mid X=x]
\]

the probability of receiving treatment. For each patient, the corresponding residuals are

\[
\tilde Y_i = Y_i - \hat m(X_i), \qquad
\tilde T_i = T_i - \hat e(X_i).
\]

The outcome residual $\tilde Y_i$ represents the part of the observed outcome that is not
explained by the patient's baseline characteristics, while $\tilde T_i$ represents the part
of treatment assignment not explained by those same characteristics. The forest therefore
searches for treatment-effect heterogeneity using these residual quantities rather than the
raw outcome and treatment variables.

Each tree recursively partitions patients according to their covariates $X$, looking for
splits that separate regions with different treatment effects. Within each resulting leaf,
the local treatment effect is estimated from the relationship between the residualized
outcome and residualized treatment,

\[
\tilde Y_i \approx \tau(x)\,\tilde T_i.
\]

The coefficient $\tau(x)$ therefore represents the local treatment effect for patients
belonging to that region of the covariate space. Across the forest, a patient falls into
different leaves in different trees, and the corresponding local treatment-effect estimates
are aggregated to obtain the final CATE estimate

\[
\hat\tau(x).
\]

In an observational study, residualizing treatment assignment is important because the
probability of receiving treatment may depend on $X$, potentially introducing confounding.
In MASTER DAPT, however, treatment is randomized, so the propensity score is fixed by
design and does not systematically depend on patient characteristics. The treatment residual
therefore mainly represents the randomized deviation from the treatment-assignment
probability.

Residualizing the outcome remains useful even under randomization. By subtracting the
component of $Y$ that can already be predicted from $X$, the model removes prognostic
variation that is unrelated to treatment. This is analogous to covariate adjustment in a
randomized trial: it does not change the validity of the treatment comparison, but can
improve its precision by allowing the forest to focus more directly on variation in the
treatment effect.

A second and separate protection against overfitting is provided by \emph{honest splitting}.
The observations used to determine where a tree should split are separated from those used
to estimate the treatment effect within the resulting leaves. The forest therefore cannot
use the same random outcome fluctuation both to define a subgroup and to estimate an
apparently different treatment effect within that subgroup.

This distinction remains important even in a randomized trial. Randomization protects the
treatment comparison from confounding, whereas honest splitting protects the estimated
heterogeneity from adaptive overfitting.

\paragraph{Bayesian causal forest (BCF).}
A Bayesian counterpart \citep{hahn2020bcf} writes the outcome as
\[
Y_i(t) = \mu(X_i) + \tau(X_i)\, t + \varepsilon_i,
\]
separating the prognostic surface $\mu(x)$, a patient's baseline risk
independent of treatment, from the effect surface $\tau(x)$ itself, and
placing a separate prior and a separate tree ensemble on each term. A
stronger, more differentiated prior on the prognostic side reduces the
extent to which a residual confounder with a large main effect can be
mistaken for treatment-effect heterogeneity; the trade-off is that the
effect surface can, in turn, be dominated by its own regularization if
that prior is too tight. BCF returns a full posterior over $\tau(x)$
rather than a point estimate, which later sections use to assess not
only the magnitude of the estimated effect but the uncertainty attached
to it.

\paragraph{In-context model (CausalPFN).}
Rather than being fitted to one dataset, this estimator is pre-trained once on
a large distribution of synthetic causal problems \citep{balazadeh2025causalpfn} and, at inference time,
maps the whole cohort and a query point directly to a prediction,
\[
(\{X_i,T_i,Y_i\}_{i=1}^n,\, x) \;\longmapsto\; \hat\tau(x),
\]
without any further fitting: the training cohort plays the role of the
prompt a language model conditions on. Its errors
therefore have a different origin from the other four: not sampling variance
from a specific, finite cohort, but a domain bias inherited from the
distribution of synthetic problems it was pre-trained on. That difference in
kind is what makes it a genuinely independent check rather than one more
variant of the same idea.

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{Interaction forest (S-learner).}
The S-learner of the same taxonomy \citep{salditt2024}: fits a single classification forest $\hat f(x,t,x\cdot t)$ on the augmented
design $[X, T, X\cdot T]$, covariates, treatment, and their interactions, and
recovers the implied CATE as
\[
\hat\tau(x) = \hat f(x,1,x\cdot 1) - \hat f(x,0,x\cdot 0),
\]
toggling the treatment column and differencing the two predictions. Because both treatment arms are fitted jointly,
information is pooled across them, which can help stabilize estimation in
the arm with fewer events. This pooling, however, can limit the detection of
treatment-effect heterogeneity: interaction effects must compete for splits
with the treatment's main effect and the prognostic signal in $X$, so weak
interactions may be attenuated toward zero rather than clearly identified.

\vspace{10pt}

None of the five is included because it is the best estimator in the
abstract — under different data each could plausibly outperform the others.
They are included because their failure modes do not overlap: two-model
error compounding, a forest's limited splitting language, a Bayesian model's
own regularization, a pre-trained model's domain bias, and an interaction
model's shrinkage toward zero are five different ways of being wrong, and an
agreement that survives all five is no longer explainable by any single one
of them.

\section{The Ranking as the Object of Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Aggregation gives back what the missing counterfactual takes away, but only
once there are groups to aggregate over, and nothing in the data defines
them. The estimator itself supplies the grouping: ordering patients by their
predicted $\tau(x)$ turns a quantity attached to individuals into a family of
nested subgroups — the top 5\%, the top 10\%, and so on — each of which has a
group average treatment effect that is estimable. What is tested is then not
the value assigned to any patient but a property of the ordering: the
patients the model places at the top must, as a group, actually show a larger
effect than those it places below. How large that effect is, is a separate
question, asked and answered separately.

\vspace{10pt}

The group averages are built from a \emph{doubly robust} pseudo-outcome \citep{kennedy2020}, one
number per patient, formed by taking the prediction of an outcome model and
correcting it with the inverse-propensity-weighted residual of the arm the
patient was actually assigned to. Individually this number is far too noisy
to be read as that patient's treatment effect — it is not an estimate of
$\tau(x)$ and is never used as one — but its average over any subgroup is a
consistent estimate of that subgroup's average effect, and it stays
consistent if either the outcome model or the treatment-assignment model is
correctly specified. Here the second is correct by construction: treatment
was allocated at random, so the assignment probability is known rather than
estimated, and the validity of every group average that follows does not rest
on the outcome model being right.

\begin{equation}
\hat{\varphi}_i \;=\; \hat{\mu}(1, X_i) - \hat{\mu}(0, X_i)
\;+\; \frac{T_i - \hat{e}(X_i)}{\hat{e}(X_i)\big(1 - \hat{e}(X_i)\big)}
\Big(Y_i - \hat{\mu}(T_i, X_i)\Big)
\label{eq:aipw-pseudo-outcome}
\end{equation}

\noindent where
\begin{description}
    \item[$X_i$] covariate vector for patient $i$;
    \item[$T_i \in \{0,1\}$] treatment actually received by patient $i$ (e.g. $1$ = prolonged DAPT, $0$ = abbreviated DAPT);
    \item[$Y_i$] observed outcome for patient $i$;
    \item[$\hat{\mu}(t, x)$] outcome model prediction for treatment arm $t$ given covariates $x$;
    \item[$\hat{e}(x)$] propensity score, $P(T=1 \mid X=x)$; known by design under randomization rather than estimated;
\end{description}

\vspace{10pt}

The \textbf{TOC curve} (targeting operator characteristic) reads the ordering
off at every depth. For each fraction $q$ it takes the patients ranked
highest by predicted $\tau(x)$ and reports the difference between the average effect within that
group and the average effect over the whole cohort — equivalently, the gain
over selecting a group of the same size at random. A ranking that carries no
information gives a curve flat at zero, and every curve is pinned to zero at
$q=1$, where the selected group is the cohort itself; a ranking that works
gives a curve that is positive and decreasing, since concentrating on fewer
patients should concentrate the effect. The thresholds that cut the ranking
are fixed on the data used to fit the estimator and then applied to held-out
patients, whose pseudo-outcomes supply the effects, so no part of the curve
is read off the sample that produced the ranking being tested.

\vspace{10pt}

Two scalar summaries integrate the TOC curve, differing only in how they
weight its depths. \textbf{AUTOC} weights every fraction equally, so a narrow
high-benefit group at the top contributes as much as a broad one; it is the
more powerful summary when the benefit is concentrated in a small subgroup.
\textbf{QINI} \citep{radcliffe2007} weights each point by the size of the group it refers to, which
suppresses the top of the ranking and rewards an effect spread broadly across
the population. Which of the two is the appropriate lens depends on whether
the responsive subgroup is a narrow tail or a large fraction of the cohort —
precisely what is not known before the analysis — so both are reported, and
only a conclusion that holds under both weightings is a property of the
ranking rather than of the choice of weight.

\vspace{10pt}

A second test asks the question the ordering deliberately sets aside.
Grouping patients into quantiles of predicted CATE, \emph{group calibration}
\citep{dwivedi2020} compares the effect actually estimated within each group, $\text{GATE}_g^{DR}$,
against the effect the model predicts for that same group, $\bar\tau_g$, and
scales the discrepancy by how far the group effects lie from the overall
average effect $\hat\theta_{\text{ATE}}$,
\[
R^2_{\text{cal}} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{G} p_g \left|\text{GATE}_g^{DR} - \bar\tau_g\right|}{\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{G} p_g \left|\text{GATE}_g^{DR} - \hat\theta_{\text{ATE}}\right|},
\]
where $g$ indexes the $G$ quantile groups, $p_g$ is the fraction of patients
in group $g$, $\text{GATE}_g^{DR}$ is the average doubly robust pseudo-outcome
within the group, $\bar\tau_g$ is the average predicted effect within the
group, and $\hat\theta_{\text{ATE}}$ is the doubly robust estimate of the
average treatment effect over the whole cohort --- the effect a model that
assigns every patient the cohort average would predict. The resulting
calibration $R^2$ falls to zero or below exactly when the model does no
better than that constant baseline. The two tests therefore fail in different
ways: a model can be well calibrated and still order nobody usefully, if the
effect it correctly predicts is the same for everyone, and it can order
patients correctly while overstating by a constant factor how much they
differ.

\vspace{10pt}

A complementary check is the \textbf{best linear predictor (BLP) slope}
\citep{chernozhukov2022}. Regressing the doubly robust pseudo-outcome
$\hat\varphi_i$ on the model's predicted effect $\hat\tau(x_i)$,
\[
\hat\varphi_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\hat\tau(x_i) + \epsilon_i,
\]
a slope $\beta_1$ significantly different from zero indicates that the
predicted ranking tracks real variation in the observed effect; a slope
near zero or negative indicates that it does not. Unlike the TOC-based
summaries, which test only whether the ranking concentrates effect at the
top, the BLP slope asks whether the predicted CATE is linearly informative
about the true effect across its whole range. TOC, AUTOC, QINI and the BLP
slope are computed together, on the same held-out pseudo-outcomes, by the
doubly robust tester used throughout this thesis
(\texttt{econml.validate.DRTester}).

\section{The Noise Floor}

A positive AUTOC, or any nonzero dispersion of the estimated $\tau(x)$ across
patients, is not by itself evidence of heterogeneity: an estimator applied to
a constant true effect still produces a spread of estimates, because
estimation error alone has variance, and that spread grows with how flexible
the model is allowed to be, independently of whether the underlying effect
varies at all. Comparing against zero therefore answers the wrong question.

\vspace{10pt}

The comparison used throughout this thesis is against a \textbf{noise floor}
obtained by permuting the treatment labels and repeating the identical
estimation and evaluation pipeline. Permutation destroys any real treatment
effect while leaving the covariates, the outcome rates, and every modelling
choice untouched, so the resulting distribution of AUTOC — or of CATE
dispersion — is exactly what the pipeline manufactures from nothing. A result
is treated as a signal only if it clears this floor not zero.

\section{When One Outcome Is Not Enough}
\label{sec:multi-outcome}

Everything so far ranks patients by the CATE of a single outcome. When only
one outcome matters, that ranking is sufficient on its own: selecting the
patients with the highest estimated $\tau(x)$ is enough to guide any
downstream decision, whether it is enrollment or treatment assignment.

\vspace{10pt}

Clinical decisions rarely reduce to one outcome. A treatment that reduces
one type of event can simultaneously increase another, and ranking patients
separately by each outcome's CATE can recommend opposite actions for the
same patient. Optimizing one target in isolation can therefore be
detrimental to another, so a joint criterion is needed whenever more than
one outcome is at stake.

\vspace{10pt}

A convenient way to represent this trade-off is a two-dimensional plane,
with the CATE of one outcome on one axis and the CATE of a second outcome,
or a weighted combination of several, on the other, both oriented so that a
positive value favours the same treatment arm. Once both axes share that
convention, the plane splits into four zones with fixed clinical meanings: a
win--win quadrant, where that arm improves both outcomes and there is
nothing to trade; a lose--lose quadrant, where the opposite arm is
preferable on both; and two off-diagonal quadrants, where the two outcomes
disagree and a genuine trade-off exists. Only in a trade-off quadrant does
the answer depend on the individual patient, which is exactly what a
personalized rule would have to find. The relative weight given to each
outcome inside such a combination is a clinical judgement, not a
statistical one, and must be supplied by domain experts together with the
reasoning behind it; the ranking machinery of this chapter is agnostic to
how that weight is chosen.

\section{From Estimation to Decision: The Value of a Rule}

A ranking can be statistically correct and still not be worth acting on if the
gain it identifies is too small to justify changing the treatment strategy.
The next step therefore asks a different question: not whether the model ranks
patients correctly, but whether \emph{following} that ranking leads to a
meaningful improvement in outcomes.

\vspace{10pt}

This requires a treatment policy that determines which treatment should be
given to each patient based on the expected balance of gains and losses.
Ranking patients using only one selected outcome may not be sufficient, since
improving one outcome can come at the cost of worsening another. The policy
must therefore consider the relevant outcomes jointly and evaluate whether
the resulting treatment decisions provide an overall benefit.

\vspace{10pt}

For each candidate policy, its expected value is estimated using a
cross-fitted AIPW (augmented inverse-propensity-weighted) estimator. The
estimated value is then compared with simple fixed strategies that do not use
the model, such as treating everyone with the same treatment. Confidence
intervals are used to assess whether the difference is meaningful. This
changes the question from \emph{`Does the model rank patients correctly?''}
to \emph{`Does following its recommendations actually improve outcomes?''}


\section{The Consequence for Model Selection}

If the quantity that matters is the quality of the ranking, the
hyperparameters of every estimator must be selected against that same
target, not against a prediction-error criterion that has no direct
bearing on it. Model selection therefore searches over hyperparameter
configurations, tree depth, regularization strength, and similar settings,
and scores each one by the cross-validated \emph{lower bound} of the AUTOC,
\[
\text{AUTOC} - z \cdot \text{SE},
\]
where $z$ is the standard-normal quantile fixing the confidence level and
$\text{SE}$ is the standard error of the AUTOC across the cross-validation
folds, rather than by the AUTOC point estimate itself. The choice is a
deliberate defence against the winner's curse: across many configurations
tried, the one that looks best often looks so partly by chance, and scoring
by the point estimate alone would systematically select the configuration
that got the luckiest draw rather than the one that generalizes best;
subtracting $z \cdot \text{SE}$ discounts a configuration whose apparently
high AUTOC rests on a single noisy fold.

\vspace{10pt}

A second rule handles one specific failure mode directly: a configuration
whose fitted $\tau(x)$ collapses to a constant, the same value for every
patient, produces no ranking at all, so its AUTOC has no meaningful
variability across folds and its standard error can be small purely by
accident. Such a configuration is not scored by the lower-bound formula but
overridden to zero, so that an accidentally small standard error cannot
substitute for an informative ranking. The search over hyperparameters
covers the nuisance models, $\hat m(x)$ and $\hat e(x)$, jointly with the
effect model, because a less noisy doubly robust pseudo-outcome raises the
achievable lower bound exactly as much as a more expressive forest does;
tuning one while holding the other fixed would credit the wrong component
for any gain.

\vspace{10pt}

The measure used to validate an estimator, the criterion used to select
among tuning configurations, and the criterion used to accept or reject a
claim of heterogeneity are, by construction, the same quantity: no separate
notion of predictive accuracy is allowed to override what the ranking-based
evaluation of \S~\ref{sec:validation} says.

\section{Chapter Summary}
This chapter contains the description of the problem clarifying why the estimation of the
benefit of the treatment can't be estimated or validated as a prediction problem.
The chapter also describes the five families of estimators used in this project, and the reasons
why they were chosen. The chapter also describes the validation strategy used to validate the ranking of the
patients by the estimated treatment effect, and the reasons why this ranking is the object of validation.
The chapter also describes the concepts of the multi-outcome ranking and the concept of a treatment policy.
Finally explains how the noise floor is used to avod false positives and how the model hyperparameters are
selected to maximize the ranking quality rather than the prediction quality.
