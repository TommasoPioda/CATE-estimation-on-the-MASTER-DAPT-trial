\section{Main Findings}

This thesis asked whether treatment-effect heterogeneity in MASTER DAPT could be
estimated reliably and then used to guide patient enrollment. The answer is mixed. The
average treatment effect is recovered consistently, but the available data do not
support a stable patient-level ranking of treatment benefit. Guided enrollment can
nevertheless select cohorts whose observed event rates differ from random enrollment,
although the differences are small, mechanism-dependent, and do not establish a
clinically useful treatment-effect trade-off.

\vspace{10pt}

Across five estimator families, prolonged therapy increases overall bleeding by
approximately $4$--$7$ percentage points, while effects on the individual ischemic
endpoints are small and generally compatible with zero (Chapter~6). The cross-fitted
AIPW analysis of NACE and MACCE leads to the same broad conclusion: abbreviated therapy
reduces bleeding, whereas no net ischemic effect is detected. Agreement with the
randomized comparison is an important positive control, but it validates average-effect
estimation rather than the more difficult task of estimating heterogeneity.

\vspace{10pt}

The direct CATE analyses provide no reproducible evidence of an exploitable treatment-
effect ranking. Estimated dispersion changes markedly with the estimator and model
flexibility, and largely overlaps the dispersion obtained after permuting treatment
labels. Held-out doubly robust validation is inconsistent across estimators and
endpoints. One nominal result, the BCF AUTOC for bleeding ($p=0.04$ in
Table~\ref{tab:drtester}), does not survive as a general finding: its sign is not shared
by the causal forest, the other ranking metrics do not provide consistent support, and
no correction was made for the many estimator--endpoint comparisons. The appropriate
conclusion is therefore that no stable and independently supported CATE ranking was
found, not that treatment effects are identical for all patients.

\vspace{10pt}

The treatment-policy analysis strengthens this practical conclusion. On NACE, neither
the policy tree nor the policy forest improves on assigning abbreviated therapy to all
patients; two learned policies are significantly worse than that fixed strategy. Within
this cohort and for this endpoint, model-based personalization therefore adds no
demonstrated clinical value over the simpler policy supported by the trial average.

\vspace{10pt}

The guided-enrollment experiments answer a related but distinct question. Several
selection rules produce cohorts with observed event rates that differ significantly
from random enrollment. Under Mechanism~2, reversing the ischemic score reverses the
change in observed ischemic rate ($+0.14$ percentage points, $p<0.001$, versus $-0.15$
percentage points, $p=0.015$). This mirror response indicates that the estimated
ischemic axis contains a
selection signal. Net-benefit also lowers the ischemic rate under Mechanisms~1, 2, 4 and
5, while some rules move bleeding as well. A CATE ranking can thus be too weak or
unstable for held-out causal validation and still be associated with cohort composition
when repeatedly used as a selection score.

\vspace{10pt}

That association should not be overinterpreted. The largest single-axis separation is
observed under Mechanism~1 with the $-$isch rule: the enrolled cohort's ischemic event
rate is $0.83$ percentage points lower than that of the left-out group ($p<0.001$).
Against random enrollment, however, the selected-cohort difference for the same rule is
$-0.10$ percentage points. The largest significant movement of both bleeding and
ischemic rates in the same comparison occurs under Mechanism~2 and remains below half a
percentage point on either axis ($-0.28$ and $-0.15$ percentage points). Directions vary
across policies and mechanisms, and some significant movements oppose the intended
clinical objective. Guided enrollment is distinguishable from random
selection in these simulations, but the thesis does not show that it identifies patients
with larger causal benefit, improves trial precision at equal sample size, or produces a
clinically preferable cohort.

\section{Interpretation of the Heterogeneity Results}

For bleeding, the minimum reliably detectable heterogeneity is approximately $0.05$ on
the absolute-risk scale, comparable to the entire average treatment effect. At
$\delta=0.02$, a still clinically relevant modifier, the protocol succeeds in 80\% of
simulations rather than the pre-specified 90\%. The data therefore argue against large,
readily exploitable bleeding-effect heterogeneity, while remaining compatible with
smaller heterogeneity.

\vspace{10pt}

For myocardial infarction and cardiovascular death, reliable detection requires injected
effect modification corresponding to odds ratios of approximately 3 and 5. With only 109
and 81 events, respectively, realistic heterogeneity is not reliably measurable. Stroke,
with 35 observed events, cannot be assessed using the present sensitivity protocol
because its null simulation is already degenerate. These endpoints should consequently
be described as underpowered or non-interpretable for heterogeneity, rather than as
showing evidence of a constant effect.

\vspace{10pt}

Two features of MASTER DAPT offer a plausible explanation for the limited signal. First,
eligibility required high bleeding risk, restricting the range of covariates from which
variation in bleeding benefit would need to be learned. This is consistent with the
modest discrimination for bleeding (AUROC 0.62 despite 506 events) compared with
cardiovascular death (AUROC 0.76 with 81 events). Second, the weighted ischemic axis
combines rare components whose estimated effects can have opposite signs, allowing
cancellation in the aggregate. These observations explain the results coherently, but
they do not prove that range restriction and aggregation are their sole causes;
unmeasured modifiers, outcome noise and limited sample size remain possible contributors.

\section{Methodological Implications}

Three lessons extend beyond this case study. First, CATE dispersion is not evidence of
heterogeneity unless it is compared with the variation generated by the same pipeline
under a null effect and validated on held-out randomized data. This is motivated by the
null simulations in this study: non-zero patient-level variation can be produced even
when treatment labels are randomized, so dispersion alone cannot distinguish signal
from estimation noise. Second, estimator agreement on the ATE cannot substitute for
validation of the patient ranking: average effects and effect modification are different
targets with very different power requirements. This is motivated by the present
results, where the estimator families consistently recovered the trial ATE but did not
produce a stable, reproducible CATE ranking. Third, selecting patients using
pre-randomization covariates preserves treatment randomization within the selected
cohort, whereas assigning treatment deterministically from the same score does not; an
allocation rule must retain positive treatment probability in both arms to preserve
identification. This distinction is motivated by causal identification: baseline-based
selection only restricts the study population, while deterministic allocation removes
the treated--control comparison for patients assigned with certainty to one arm.

\vspace{10pt}

The enrollment results add a further caution. A detectable change in the event rate of a
selected cohort does not by itself demonstrate successful causal targeting. Selection
scores may track baseline prognosis, correlated outcomes, or unstable model variation as
well as treatment-effect heterogeneity. Evaluation should therefore include both cohort
composition and an independent causal target, ideally trial precision or policy value at
a fixed enrolled sample size.

\section{Limitations}

Probably the biggest limitation of this thesis is the available data. The MASTER DAPT 
preselected high-bleeding risk patients limits the view of the population and constrained 
the models to a narrow region of the covariate space. In addition, the number of ischemic 
events is very limited, which makes it difficult to detect heterogeneity in those endpoints.
Including more patients with a wider range of baseline risk and more ischemic events would be
very beneficial to future analyses.

There is no external validation cohort. Multiple endpoints, estimators, scores and
mechanisms are compared without formal multiplicity correction, so isolated nominal
$p$-values are exploratory. The conflict subgroup of Chapter~6 is in-sample and
descriptive because the same outcomes contributed to fitting the score and defining the
subgroup.

\vspace{10pt}

The guided-enrollment experiments have design-specific limitations. Mechanism~1 is
evaluated after approximately 92\% of the cohort has been enrolled, leaving limited scope
for selection to change cohort composition. Mechanisms~2--5 operate at a smaller final
cohort but do not provide the matched-size, non-adaptive randomized-arrival baseline
needed to determine whether target precision can be reached with fewer patients. The
experiments also reuse a completed trial: they simulate who would have been enrolled
while retaining observed assignments and outcomes, rather than prospectively validating
an adaptive design.

\vspace{10pt}

Finally, the sensitivity calculation is complete only for the causal forest. Its bleeding
baseline-risk model underestimates prevalence, making the threshold conservative in an
unquantified direction, and the stroke simulation does not reproduce a usable null
distribution. The computational environment is not yet fully packaged for independent
reproduction.

\section{Further Work}

The most informative next study would apply the complete protocol to a larger cohort that
is not restricted to high-bleeding-risk patients and contains more ischemic events. Such
a dataset would test the range-restriction explanation directly and provide enough power
to evaluate ischemic components separately rather than only through a weighted aggregate.
External validation should pre-specify estimators, endpoints and multiplicity adjustment.

\vspace{10pt}

Within the present data, the sensitivity analysis should be extended to all five
estimators, the bleeding prevalence model recalibrated, and the rare-event simulation
redesigned before reporting a stroke threshold. Guided enrollment should be compared at
earlier checkpoints against a matched-size random-arrival baseline, using precision of
the treatment-effect estimate as the primary outcome. This would answer the original
design question: whether guided enrollment reaches the same inferential precision with
fewer patients, rather than only whether it changes cohort composition.

\section{Conclusion}

MASTER DAPT provides strong evidence for an average bleeding benefit of abbreviated DAPT,
but this thesis finds no stable evidence that the magnitude of that causal benefit can be
ranked reliably across individual patients using the available baseline covariates. Large
bleeding-effect heterogeneity is not supported; smaller heterogeneity remains possible,
and heterogeneity in the rare ischemic endpoints is not measurable with adequate power.

\vspace{10pt}

Guided enrollment is not equivalent to random enrollment: several mechanisms produce
small but reproducible changes in observed cohort event rates, and the ischemic mirror
test shows that at least one estimated axis carries selection signal. However, these
changes do not establish causal targeting, clinical benefit, or improved trial
efficiency. The central result is therefore neither that personalization is impossible
nor that guided enrollment succeeds, but that reliable personalization requires a
population with sufficient risk variation, enough endpoint events, and validation
criteria aligned with the decision the model is intended to support.
