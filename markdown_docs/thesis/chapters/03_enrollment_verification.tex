The previous chapter developed the theory behind ranking patients by their estimated
CATE. This chapter turns that ranking into a scoring process for enrollment itself,
selecting from the cohort the patients who gain most from the treatment, on the basis of
the ranking theory just defined.

\vspace{5pt}

\textit{If that ranking is credible, could it be used during the enrollment process to select the patients that are more likely to benefit from the treatment?}

\vspace{5pt}

A credible ranking is not automatically a safe one to enroll by: the same selection that
makes the ranking useful can also damage the trial it is meant to serve. Choosing the
right metric and the right policy is what keeps the ranking credible once it starts
shaping who is enrolled, and the aim of this chapter is to identify a combination that
gives the best possible outcome across the trial's simulations.

\section{The Trade-off Plane, Applied to MASTER DAPT}

Bleeding and ischemic risk are the two outcomes that must be traded off in
MASTER DAPT (\S~\ref{sec:multi-outcome}). The bleeding axis is the CATE of
major bleeding under abbreviated therapy, a single scalar quantity by
construction. The ischemic axis combines myocardial infarction, stroke and
cardiovascular death into a single weighted score, with weights $0.4$ for
MI, $0.4$ for stroke and $0.2$ for cardiovascular death.

Both axes carry the effect of \emph{abbreviating} the therapy, so a positive
value on either axis always favours abbreviation
(Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff-zones}). In the bottom-right quadrant, abbreviating
reduces bleeding but increases ischemic risk: this is the only quadrant where
the answer depends on the individual patient, and it is the one a
personalized enrollment rule would have to identify.

\section{Five Mechanisms, from the Ideal Ceiling to the Realistic Case}

A score defines a preference order over patients, but it does not say how that
order is consulted. Two questions remain open once the score is fixed, and
neither has an answer that follows from the score itself. The first is how many
candidates are visible at the moment a decision must be taken: the whole
population, a handful, or a single pair. The second is whether the model that
produces the score already exists, or has to be learned from the very cohort it
is being used to build.

\vspace{10pt}
An \emph{enrollment mechanism} is a procedure that translates the score output by the
treatment-effect model into an actual enrollment decision. It must specify which
patients are available to be compared at each step, and how the model is updated as new
patients get enrolled.
\vspace{10pt}

Five mechanisms are used in this work, forming a ladder from the ideal case to the
realistic one rather than five competing proposals of which one is meant to win.
\emph{The ideal ceiling} (Mechanism~1) sees the entire remaining pool at every decision
and enrolls the best-scoring patients outright. \emph{Partially random enrollment}
(Mechanism~2) narrows that view to a random sample redrawn at each round. \emph{The
duel} (Mechanism~3), \emph{UCB enrollment} (Mechanism~4) and \emph{Thompson enrollment}
(Mechanism~5) narrow it further, down to a single pair; the latter two resolve that pair
with the model's own uncertainty rather than the score alone. The first assumes
everything an enrollment could possibly know; the last assumes only what a real one has.

\paragraph{What all five share.}
Every mechanism runs the same skeleton. A \emph{seed} cohort is drawn uniformly
at random and the effect model is fitted on it, because before any patient is
enrolled there is no model with which to choose one. Candidates are then
presented according to the mechanism's own rule and scored by the model as it
currently stands. The winning candidate enters the cohort with the treatment
they actually received and the outcome they actually had --- no counterfactual
is ever invented. The candidate not chosen is not returned to the pool for a
later comparison: each patient is proposed at most once over the course of a
run, so being passed over is a permanent exclusion from the enrolled cohort,
not a deferral. The model is refitted every $r$ enrollments, so that each
decision is taken by a model that has already learned from every enrollment
before it.

\paragraph{The seed, and why nothing is tuned after it.}
The seed is the last cohort drawn at random. After that, patients are selected
according to the score. Since the score depends only on the covariates,
selection does not break identification: treatment remains randomized within
the enrolled cohort, so the doubly robust methods of Chapter~2 remain valid.
What changes is the distribution of the covariates. The enrolled patients are
therefore a valid sample, but from a different population created by the
selection mechanism itself.

\vspace{10pt}

For this reason, hyperparameter tuning is performed only on the seed. Tuning
on a later cohort would estimate a valid AUTOC, but not the same AUTOC across
configurations. If we were to tune on the cohort selected during the run,
each configuration would be evaluated on the population that was able to select.
One configuration could therefore appear better simply
because it produced an easier cohort, rather than because it produced a better
ranking.

\vspace{10pt}

Selecting patients with high
scores can reduce the variation in treatment effects within the enrolled
cohort, which lowers AUTOC. At the same time, the selected cohort may contain
more patients in regions where the model is well supported by the data, making
the ranking appear more accurate and increasing AUTOC. Cross-validation does
not solve this problem because it is performed within the same selected
cohort: every fold has already passed through the selection mechanism.

\vspace{10pt}

The problem is more serious in the online setting. A poor configuration does
not only produce a poor model; it also determines which patients are enrolled.
If that selected cohort is then used for further tuning, the model and the
population influence each other. There is no randomly selected test cohort
available to provide an independent reference.

\vspace{10pt}

Tuning is therefore performed once on the seed, using the same budget for every
mechanism, and the resulting configuration is kept fixed throughout the run.
This ensures that the mechanisms start from comparable models and that later
differences are due to their selection rules rather than to different tuning
procedures. The remaining limitation is that the seed is relatively small, so
the AUTOC estimate is noisy and the tuning procedure may still select a
configuration that performs well partly by chance.


\paragraph{Mechanism 1: the ideal ceiling.}
At every round this mechanism scores the entire not-yet-enrolled pool with the current
model and enrolls the highest-scoring patients outright; the model is then refit on the
enlarged cohort and the next round repeats the same ranking over whoever remains.
Because no other mechanism below is ever shown more of the pool at once, it provides an
upper bound on their performance and is used as a reference to evaluate them. It is also
the natural setting in which to compare candidate scores or ranking rules against each
other, since the ranking itself is never distorted by a restricted view of the pool. A
stochastic variant of the same ranking, annealing from randomized toward greedy as
enrollment proceeds, is discussed alongside the other mechanisms' own uncertainty rules
below.

\paragraph{Mechanism 2: the partially random enrollment.}
At every round a random sample is drawn from the remaining pool and ranked by the
current model's score. The better half of the sample is enrolled; the worse half is
discarded permanently, not returned to the pool for a later round. The model is then
refit on the enlarged cohort and the next round draws a fresh sample. This mechanism is
more realistic than the ideal ceiling, since it never needs the score of the whole
population at once, but it is still far from a real enrollment process, because a fresh
random sample has to be assembled and scored before every round's decision.

\paragraph{Mechanism 3: the duel.}
At every step two patients are drawn from the remaining pool; the model is refit every
$r$ enrollments. How the pair is resolved, together with the uncertainty rule this
mechanism uses, is described below once all five mechanisms are on the table.

\paragraph{Mechanism 4: UCB enrollment.}

UCB1, \emph{Upper Confidence Bound}, variant 1 (Auer, Cesa-Bianchi \& Fischer, 2002),
keeps the duel of Mechanism~3 and changes only how the pair is resolved: an additive
exploration bonus on top of the score, described below alongside the other mechanisms'
uncertainty rules.

\vspace{10pt}

One point must be stated plainly, because the name suggests more than the setting
delivers. This is not a bandit in the textbook sense: an arm is never pulled twice, each
patient arrives once, and the reward is never observed, since the reward would be the
individual treatment effect, which is precisely the quantity that Chapter~2 has
established is unobservable. What is being explored is a region of the covariate space
rather than an arm, and the feedback arrives only indirectly, through the refit: the
enrolled patient contributes their real outcome to the next model, which then reports a
smaller uncertainty in that region. The optimism-under-uncertainty rule is therefore
borrowed as a selection heuristic, not as an algorithm whose regret guarantees carry over.

\paragraph{Mechanism 5: Thompson enrollment.}
Same duel as Mechanism~3, same two frames read from the current model, but the
trade-off between exploration and exploitation is resolved by sampling instead of by an
additive bonus, as described below alongside the other mechanisms' uncertainty rules.

\vspace{10pt}

Two limitations are declared here rather than discovered later. First, the normal
distribution is a working approximation and not a posterior: the quantity used as its
width is a frequentist standard error of the forest, and treating it as the spread of a
belief is a modelling choice, defensible but not derived. Second, whether the mechanism
actually explores is an empirical question and not a property of the rule: if the
sampling scale is small relative to the spread of the scores, the draws almost always
order the pair the same way the scores already did, and Thompson degenerates towards
Mechanism~3. The check is direct, and it is carried out with the mechanisms: compare the
average uncertainty of the enrolled cohort against that of the random control arm. A
mechanism that claims to explore and enrolls patients no more uncertain than a coin flip
would is not exploring.

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{The refit cadence.}
Mechanisms 3, 4 and 5 share one more parameter: the refit cadence $r$, the number of
enrollments between one fit of the model and the next; Mechanisms 1 and 2 refit every
round by construction, since their round is already the batch being decided. The cadence
is a computational choice rather than a statistical one, but it must be reported all the
same, because it changes what each mechanism actually is: a mechanism that refits after every single
enrollment and one that refits after a large batch are not the same mechanism, since the
second spends long stretches of decisions guided by a model that is already stale.
Batching itself is not a shortcut invented for this thesis --- a real adaptive trial
would also retrain on batches rather than after every single participant, both for
logistical reasons and because re-randomizing the assignment rule after each individual
enrollment is itself a source of instability. What is specific to this work is the size
of $r$: computational cost keeps it larger here than an operational trial would need, so
the model guiding enrollment lags further behind what a smaller, more frequent batch
would achieve. Any comparison between a guided mechanism and the random control arm is
therefore made with the guided arm carrying this handicap, on top of whatever its own
selection rule costs it.

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{How each mechanism resolves uncertainty.}
A mechanism is fixed by two independent choices: how many candidates are visible at each
decision, already described for each mechanism above, and how that choice folds in the
model's own uncertainty once those candidates are in view. Three rules cover every use
of the second choice across the five mechanisms, and each pairs naturally with a
particular visibility scheme. Write $s(x)$ for the trade-off score of the current model
and $u(x)$ for its own uncertainty on patient $x$: the standard error the forest reports
on the estimated effect, composited across the endpoints and standardized on the same
z-scored scale as $s(x)$, so the two are commensurable. The simplest rule ignores $u(x)$
entirely and keeps the higher-scoring candidate; this is the default for Mechanism~1 and
for Mechanism~2, whose own exploration weight (below) is left at zero throughout this
work.

\vspace{10pt}

\emph{Decaying exploration probability} (Mechanism~3, and Mechanism~1's stochastic
variant) overrides the score with a schedule that starts exploratory and decays toward
zero as enrollment proceeds. Applied to a pair (Mechanism~3), with probability
$p(n) = \max(e^{-n/300}, 0.05)$, where $n$ counts patients enrolled past the seed, the
pair is resolved by keeping whichever candidate carries the larger $u(x)$ instead of the
higher-scoring one; the rest of the time the score decides. Applied to the whole visible
pool at once (Mechanism~1's stochastic variant), an analogous schedule acts as a
temperature on the ranking instead of an override probability: while it is high,
patients enter the batch with a chance related to their score rather than strictly by
rank, so a lower-scoring candidate can still be enrolled; as the temperature decays to
zero the batch converges to Mechanism~1's default, greedy ranking. Both forms converge to
pure exploitation as enrollment proceeds; only the size of the visible group, and the
exact form the decay takes, changes how that convergence is carried out.

\vspace{10pt}

\emph{Additive exploration bonus} (Mechanisms~2 and~4):
\[
  \text{score}(x) \;=\; s(x) \;+\; c \cdot u(x),
\]
a fixed weight $c$ added on top of the score. The bonus is floored at zero, so a patient
the model is \emph{more} confident about than the cohort average receives no bonus, never
a penalty. $c$ is the price paid for exploration: Mechanism~4 pays it on every duel,
while Mechanism~2 sets it to zero and falls back to the score-only rule above on the
sample it draws each round. The rule is deterministic: for a given pair or sample the
same candidate always wins, so a candidate that trails on the score can only win by
having a large enough $u(x)$ to close the gap, every time.

\vspace{10pt}

For each candidate, \emph{Thompson sampling} (Mechanism~5) draws one value from
$\mathcal{N}(s(x), u(x))$, the model's belief about the
patient's value with its own uncertainty as the width of that belief, and enrolls the
candidate with the higher draw. The scale is floored at a small $\varepsilon$, since a
standardized uncertainty can be zero or negative and every candidate must still have a
valid, if narrow, distribution to sample from. Unlike the additive bonus, this rule is
not deterministic: a trailing candidate wins a fraction of the time proportional to how
much the two distributions overlap, so exploration is spread over many marginal
decisions rather than concentrated on the pair with the largest gap. Two runs over the
same arrival sequence therefore produce different cohorts; this is a property to report
rather than a defect, and one more reason every mechanism is evaluated over many
replicated cohorts instead of a single run.

\vspace{10pt}

Put together, the five mechanisms are five combinations of a visibility scheme and an
uncertainty rule:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mechanism~1: the whole remaining pool, score-only by default, with a
  decaying-temperature variant.
  \item Mechanism~2: a random sample of the pool, score-only in the results reported
  here, built on the same additive-bonus rule as Mechanism~4.
  \item Mechanism~3: a pair, decaying exploration probability.
  \item Mechanism~4: a pair, additive exploration bonus.
  \item Mechanism~5: a pair, Thompson sampling.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{Methodology of comparison.}
To evaluate the five models produced by the mechanisms, we must use an independent reference point.
The model that guides selection updates as new data arrive, so it cannot 
also be the reference point by which one judges whether the cohort has moved.
Even comparing the mechanisms against each other is not enough, because they all share the same
bias.
To have a reference point that is independent we decided to use the model fitted and hypertuned
on the entire cohort, which is the best model we could obtain from the data.

The performances of the mechanisms are evaluated by comparing the outcome-event rates of
the enrolled cohort against the discarded one, on each axis that defines the trade-off
quadrant (\S~\ref{sec:multi-outcome}). A mechanism that genuinely concentrates the
cohort on that quadrant should separate the two cohorts on both axes; one that merely
reproduces the population baseline should not. Due to the seed- and luck-based nature of
the enrollment process, a single run would not be enough to evaluate the performances of
the mechanisms, so the enrollment process is repeated $N$ times for each mechanism and
the results are averaged; Chapter~\ref{ch:guided-enrollment-applied} reports the exact
value of $N$ used for MASTER DAPT, together with the resulting comparison of enrolled
versus discarded event rates.
