The previous chapter developed the theory behind ranking patients by their estimated
CATE. This chapter turns that ranking into a scoring process for enrollment itself,
selecting from the cohort the patients who gain most from the treatment, on the basis of
the ranking theory just defined.

\vspace{5pt}

\textit{If that ranking is credible, could it be used during the enrollment process to select the patients that are more likely to benefit from the treatment?}

\vspace{5pt}

A credible ranking is not automatically a safe one to enroll by: the same selection that
makes the ranking useful can also damage the trial it is meant to serve. Choosing the
right metric and the right policy is what keeps the ranking credible once it starts
shaping who is enrolled, and the aim of this chapter is to identify a combination that
gives the best possible outcome across the trial's simulations.

\section{The Trade-off Plane, Applied to MASTER DAPT}

Bleeding and ischemic risk are the two outcomes that must be traded off in
MASTER DAPT (\S~\ref{sec:multi-outcome}). The bleeding axis is the CATE of
major bleeding under abbreviated therapy, a single scalar quantity by
construction. The ischemic axis combines myocardial infarction, stroke and
cardiovascular death into a single weighted score, with weights $0.4$ for
MI, $0.4$ for stroke and $0.2$ for cardiovascular death,
weights were chosen arbitrarily to reflect the relative severity of the three events.
Before the two axes are combined into any of the scores below, each raw CATE column ---
bleeding and the three ischemic endpoints --- is rescaled by its own interquartile range (a
\texttt{RobustScaler} with centering turned off), so that the frequent bleeding endpoint
does not dominate the rarer ischemic ones purely because of its larger raw magnitude; the
median is not subtracted, so a raw CATE of zero keeps its meaning as the "no effect"
reference on the rescaled axis as well.
Both axes carry the effect of \emph{abbreviating} the therapy, so a positive
value on either axis always favours abbreviation
(Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff-zones}). In the bottom-right quadrant, abbreviating
reduces bleeding but increases ischemic risk: this is the only quadrant where
the answer depends on the individual patient, and it is the one a
personalized enrollment rule would have to identify.
In this quadrant and in the second one the decision is not obvious because the two outcomes
favour opposite treatments, and the patient is left with a trade-off between
the two outcomes. In the first and fourth quadrants, the decision is obvious because both outcomes
favour the same treatment, and the patient is left with a clear choice.

\section{Five Mechanisms, from the Ideal Ceiling to the Realistic Case}

A score defines a preference order over patients, but it does not say how that
order is consulted. Two questions remain open once the score is fixed, and
neither has an answer that follows from the score itself. The first is how many
candidates are visible at the moment a decision must be taken: the whole
population, a handful, or a single pair. The second is whether the model that
produces the score already exists, or has to be learned from the very cohort it
is being used to build.

\vspace{10pt}
An \emph{enrollment mechanism} is a procedure that translates the score output by the
treatment-effect model into an actual enrollment decision. It must specify which
patients are available to be compared at each step, and how the model is updated as new
patients get enrolled.
\vspace{10pt}

The five mechanisms below sit within the broader literature on sequentially
choosing which unit to observe next: active adaptive experimental design for
treatment-effect estimation \citep{kato2024}, whose joint view of covariate
density and propensity score motivates treating enrolment itself as a
decision rather than a fixed protocol, and stream-based active learning more
generally, where each candidate is seen once and must be accepted or passed
over on the spot rather than compared against a stored pool
\citep{cacciarelli2023survey,cacciarelli2022stream,cacciarelli2022soft}.
Mechanisms~4 and~5's budget-limited, uncertainty-aware selection is the same
problem optimal-design and thresholding approaches to online active linear
regression address \citep{fontaine2020,riquelme2016}, adapted here to a
forest-based effect model rather than a linear one.
\vspace{10pt}

Five mechanisms are used in this work, forming a ladder from the full-pool reference to the
realistic one rather than five competing proposals of which one is meant to win.
\emph{The full-pool information reference} (Mechanism~1) sees the entire remaining pool at every decision
and, in its greedy form, enrolls the best-scoring patients outright. \emph{Partially random enrollment}
(Mechanism~2) narrows that view to a random sample redrawn at each round. \emph{The
duel} (Mechanism~3), \emph{UCB-style enrollment} (Mechanism~4) and \emph{Thompson-style enrollment}
(Mechanism~5) narrow it further, down to a single pair; Mechanism~3 sometimes resolves the
pair by uncertainty, whereas the latter two incorporate it on every decision. The first assumes
everything an enrollment could possibly know; the last assumes only what a real one has.

\paragraph{What all five share.}
Every mechanism runs the same skeleton. A \emph{seed} cohort is drawn uniformly
at random and the effect model is fitted on it, because before any patient is
enrolled there is no model with which to choose one. Candidates are then
presented according to the mechanism's own rule and scored by the model as it
currently stands. The winning candidate enters the cohort with the treatment
they actually received and the outcome they actually had --- no counterfactual
is ever invented. For Mechanisms 2 through 5, the candidate not chosen is not
returned to the pool for a later comparison: each patient is proposed at most
once over the course of a run, so being passed over is a permanent exclusion
from the enrolled cohort, not a deferral. Mechanism 1 is the exception:
because it re-scores the entire remaining pool every round rather than a
fixed sample or pair, a patient left out of one round's batch is simply
re-scored in the next round and remains eligible until the run ends, so being
passed over there is not permanent. The model is refitted every $r$ enrollments. Each refit incorporates every enrollment
observed up to that point; decisions made between refits use that fixed model and can
therefore lag behind the accumulated cohort by at most $r-1$ enrollments.

\paragraph{The seed, and why nothing is tuned after it.}
The seed is the last cohort drawn at random. After that, patients are selected
according to the score. Since the score depends only on the covariates,
selection does not break identification: treatment remains randomized within
the enrolled cohort, so the doubly robust methods of Chapter~2 remain valid.
What changes is the distribution of the covariates. The enrolled patients are
therefore a valid sample, but from a different population created by the
selection mechanism itself.

\vspace{10pt}

For this reason, hyperparameter tuning is performed only on the seed. Tuning
on a later cohort would estimate a valid AUTOC, but not the same AUTOC across
configurations. If we were to tune on the cohort selected during the run,
each configuration would be evaluated on the population that was able to select.
One configuration could therefore appear better simply
because it produced an easier cohort, rather than because it produced a better
ranking.

\vspace{10pt}

Selecting patients with high
scores can reduce the variation in treatment effects within the enrolled
cohort, which lowers AUTOC. At the same time, the selected cohort may contain
more patients in regions where the model is well supported by the data, making
the ranking appear more accurate and increasing AUTOC. Cross-validation does
not solve this problem because it is performed within the same selected
cohort: every fold has already passed through the selection mechanism.

\vspace{10pt}

The problem is more serious in the online setting. A poor configuration does
not only produce a poor model; it also determines which patients are enrolled.
If that selected cohort is then used for further tuning, the model and the
population influence each other. There is no randomly selected test cohort
available to provide an independent reference.

\vspace{10pt}

Tuning is therefore performed once on the seed, using the same budget for every
mechanism, and the resulting configuration is kept fixed throughout the run.
This ensures that the mechanisms start from comparable models and that later
differences are due to their selection rules rather than to different tuning
procedures. The remaining limitation is that the seed is relatively small, so
the AUTOC estimate is noisy and the tuning procedure may still select a
configuration that performs well partly by chance.


\paragraph{Mechanism 1: the full-pool information reference.}
At every round this mechanism scores the entire not-yet-enrolled pool with the current
model, selects a batch, refits on the enlarged cohort, and repeats over whoever remains.
Greedy selection enrolls the highest-scoring patients outright; the reported analyses use
the annealed Gumbel sampling specified below instead.
Because no other mechanism below is ever shown more of the pool at once, it provides an
information ceiling and is used as a reference to evaluate them; this wider visibility
does not guarantee a mathematical upper bound on realized performance. It is also
the natural setting in which to compare candidate scores or ranking rules against each
other, since the ranking itself is never distorted by a restricted view of the pool. A
stochastic form of the same ranking, annealing from randomized toward greedy as
enrollment proceeds, is discussed alongside the other mechanisms' selection rules
below.

\paragraph{Mechanism 2: the partially random enrollment.}
At every round a random sample is drawn from the remaining pool and ranked by the
current model's score. The better half of the sample is enrolled; the worse half is
discarded permanently, not returned to the pool for a later round. The model is then
refit on the enlarged cohort and the next round draws a fresh sample. This mechanism is
more realistic than the full-pool reference, since it never needs the score of the whole
population at once, but it is still far from a real enrollment process, because a fresh
random sample has to be assembled and scored before every round's decision.

\paragraph{Mechanism 3: the duel.}
At the start of each run the remaining pool is randomly permuted; at every step the next
two patients in that fixed arrival stream form a pair. The model is refit every
$r$ enrollments. How the pair is resolved, together with the uncertainty rule this
mechanism uses, is described below once all five mechanisms are on the table.

\paragraph{Mechanism 4: UCB-style uncertainty-bonus enrollment.}

This mechanism is inspired by the optimism-under-uncertainty principle of
upper-confidence-bound methods \citep{auer2002}. It keeps the duel of Mechanism~3 and
changes only how the pair is resolved: an additive exploration bonus on top of the score,
described below alongside the other mechanisms' uncertainty rules.

\vspace{10pt}

The distinction matters in this setting. This is not UCB1 in the classical multi-armed
bandit sense: an arm is never pulled twice, each
patient arrives once, and the reward is never observed, since the reward would be the
individual treatment effect, which is precisely the quantity that Chapter~2 has
established is unobservable. What is being explored is a region of the covariate space
rather than an arm, and the feedback arrives only indirectly, through the refit: the
enrolled patient contributes their real outcome to the next model, which then reports a
smaller uncertainty in that region. The optimism-under-uncertainty rule is therefore
borrowed as a selection heuristic, not as an algorithm whose regret guarantees carry over.

\paragraph{Mechanism 5: Thompson enrollment.}
Same duel as Mechanism~3, same two frames read from the current model, but the
trade-off between exploration and exploitation is resolved by sampling instead of by an
additive bonus, as described below alongside the other mechanisms' uncertainty rules.

\vspace{10pt}

Two limitations are declared here rather than discovered later. First, the normal
distribution is a working approximation and not a posterior: the quantity used as its
width is a frequentist standard error of the forest, and treating it as the spread of a
belief is a modelling choice, defensible but not derived. Second, whether the mechanism
actually explores is an empirical question and not a property of the rule: if the
sampling scale is small relative to the spread of the scores, the draws almost always
order the pair the same way the scores already did, and the rule approaches score-only
pairwise selection. The experiments below report cohort composition, not the average uncertainty
of the enrolled cohort relative to Random; that diagnostic remains a useful additional
check rather than a result reported in this thesis.

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{The refit cadence.}
Mechanisms 3, 4 and 5 share one more parameter: the refit cadence $r$, the number of
enrollments between one fit of the model and the next; Mechanisms 1 and 2 refit every
round by construction, since their round is already the batch being decided. The cadence
is a computational choice rather than a statistical one, but it must be reported all the
same, because it changes what each mechanism actually is: a mechanism that refits after every single
enrollment and one that refits after a large batch are not the same mechanism, since the
second spends long stretches of decisions guided by a model that is already stale.
Batching itself is not a shortcut invented for this thesis --- a real adaptive trial
would also retrain on batches rather than after every single participant, both for
logistical reasons and because re-randomizing the assignment rule after each individual
enrollment is itself a source of instability. What is specific to this work is the size
of $r$: computational cost keeps it larger here than an operational trial would need, so
the model guiding enrollment lags further behind what a smaller, more frequent batch
would achieve. Any comparison between a guided mechanism and the random control arm is
therefore made with the guided arm carrying this handicap, on top of whatever its own
selection rule costs it.

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{How mechanisms use (or ignore) uncertainty.}
A mechanism is fixed by two independent choices: how many candidates are visible at each
decision, already described above, and whether the rule uses the model's own uncertainty
when choosing among them. In the reported analyses, Mechanisms~3--5 use uncertainty;
Mechanism~2 is score-only; and Mechanism~1 uses stochastic sampling from the score rather
than uncertainty. Write $s(x)$ for the standardized acquisition score of the current model
and $u(x)$ for its uncertainty on patient $x$: the forest's per-patient CATE standard
errors, composited across endpoints on a z-scored scale. Concretely, the raw scalar is
$s_{\mathrm{raw}}(x)=\text{bleed}(x)-\text{isch}(x)$ for the trade-off score used by the
\emph{conflict} rule and
$s_{\mathrm{raw}}(x)=\text{bleed}(x)+\text{isch}(x)$ for the win-win score used by
\emph{net benefit}; the acquisition rule uses $s(x)=z(s_{\mathrm{raw}}(x))$.
Here $\text{bleed}(x)$ is the rescaled bleeding CATE and $\text{isch}(x)$ the same
$0.4/0.4/0.2$ weighted ischemic composite as the trade-off plane above. The two Angle rules
read the identical $(x,y)$ plane coordinates instead of collapsing them to this scalar
difference or sum, and target $\pm45^\circ$ on that plane. $u(x)$ is composited the same way:
the z-scored bleeding standard error plus the same $0.4/0.4/0.2$-weighted sum of the
z-scored ischemic standard errors. Since this composite is a z-score, it can be negative;
the mechanisms that require a non-negative bonus or sampling scale apply the safeguards
specified below.

\vspace{10pt}

\emph{Decaying uncertainty override} (Mechanism~3) replaces the score with an
uncertainty-based choice on a schedule that falls rapidly but retains a small exploration
rate. Applied to a pair, with probability
$p(n) = \max(e^{-n/300}, 0.05)$, where $n$ counts patients enrolled past the seed, the
pair is resolved by keeping whichever candidate carries the larger $u(x)$ instead of the
higher-scoring one; otherwise the score decides. Thus Mechanism~3 never becomes purely
exploitative: its uncertainty-based override remains active in 5\% of duels.

Mechanism~1 uses a different form of exploration that does \emph{not} use $u(x)$. In the
reported runs it samples batches by a Gumbel perturbation of the score ranking, with
temperature
\[
  \tau(n_{\mathrm{tot}}) = 5\exp\!\left(-8n_{\mathrm{tot}}/N\right),
\]
where $n_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is the total number enrolled and $N=4{,}579$. Its uncertainty
bonus is fixed at zero. A positive temperature permits lower-scoring candidates to enter
the batch; as it becomes small the selection approaches greedy score ranking.

\vspace{10pt}

\emph{Additive exploration bonus} (Mechanism~4, with Mechanism~2 as its score-only
special case):
\[
  \text{score}(x) \;=\; s(x) \;+\; c \cdot \max\{u(x),0\}.
\]
a fixed weight $c$ is added on top of the score. The bonus is floored at zero, so a patient
the model is \emph{more} confident about than the cohort average receives no bonus, never
a penalty. Mechanism~4 uses $c=1$ on every duel, while Mechanism~2 sets $c=0$ and falls
back to score-only selection on the sample it draws each round. The rule is deterministic:
for a given pair or sample the same candidate always wins, so a candidate that trails on
the score can only win by having a large enough $u(x)$ to close the gap, every time.

\vspace{10pt}

For each candidate, \emph{Thompson-style rule} \citep{thompson1933} (Mechanism~5) draws one value from
$\mathcal{N}(s(x),\max\{u(x),10^{-3}\})$ and enrolls the candidate with the higher draw.
The second argument is used as a sampling scale, not as a Bayesian posterior standard
deviation; it is a Thompson-style heuristic rather than classical posterior
sampling. Unlike the additive bonus, this rule is not deterministic: a trailing candidate
wins a fraction of the time proportional to how much the two sampling distributions
overlap, so exploration is spread over many marginal decisions rather than concentrated on
the pair with the largest gap. Two runs over the same arrival sequence therefore produce
different cohorts; this is a property to report rather than a defect, and one more reason
every mechanism is evaluated over many replicated cohorts instead of a single run.

\vspace{10pt}

Put together, the five mechanisms are five combinations of a visibility scheme and a
selection rule:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Mechanism~1: the whole remaining pool, Gumbel-perturbed score sampling with
  an annealed temperature and no use of $u(x)$.
  \item Mechanism~2: a random sample of the pool, score-only in the results reported
  here ($c=0$), built on the same additive-bonus form as Mechanism~4.
  \item Mechanism~3: a pair, decaying uncertainty override with a 5\% floor.
  \item Mechanism~4: a pair, additive uncertainty bonus ($c=1$).
  \item Mechanism~5: a pair, Thompson-style sampling.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{10pt}

\paragraph{Methodology of comparison.}
To evaluate the five enrollment mechanisms, we must use an independent reference point.
The model that guides selection updates as new data arrive, so it cannot
also be the reference point by which one judges whether the cohort has moved.
Even comparing the mechanisms against each other is not enough, because they all share the same
bias.
The reference point that avoids this circularity is not a model at all: the mechanisms are
evaluated on the patients' actual, observed trial outcomes.

Two complementary contrasts are used. Where a mechanism records a rejected group, the first compares the post-seed enrolled cohort
with the patients the same rule leaves out or discards, on each axis that defines the
trade-off quadrant (\S~\ref{sec:multi-outcome}); it measures whether the rule sorts the
patients it sees. The second compares that post-seed enrolled cohort directly with the
post-seed cohort produced by the mechanism-matched Random rule, paired by replication; it
answers whether guidance changes cohort composition relative to unguided enrollment. The
common random seed is excluded from these displayed groups because no policy selected its
members. Due to the seed- and luck-based nature of the enrollment process, a single run
would not be enough to evaluate performance, so the enrollment process is repeated $N$
times for each mechanism and the results are averaged; Chapter~\ref{ch:guided-enrollment-applied} reports the exact
value of $N$ used for MASTER DAPT, together with the resulting comparison of enrolled
versus discarded event rates.

\section{Chapter Summary}
This chapter contains the description of the five enrollment mechanisms 
used in this project, and the reasons why they were chosen. The chapter 
also describes the trade-off plane used to evaluate the performance of
the mechanisms, and the reasons why this plane is the object of evaluation. Finally, the chapter
explains how the mechanisms are evaluated: by comparing the outcome-event rates of the
enrolled and discarded cohorts on both axes of the trade-off quadrant, averaged over repeated
enrollment runs.
