Clinical trials are the cornerstone of evidence-based medicine. By randomly assigning
patients to competing treatment strategies and comparing their outcomes, randomized
controlled trials (RCTs) provide the most rigorous framework for establishing causal
claims about treatment efficacy. However, this statistical rigor comes at a significant
cost: large, expensive, and time-consuming experiments that often enroll thousands of
patients over several years. The ethical and logistical implications are substantial —
patients allocated to an inferior treatment bear avoidable harm, and delays in reaching
conclusions slow down the translation of effective therapies into clinical practice.
These limitations have motivated the development of adaptive trial designs, which
allow pre-planned modifications to the trial protocol as data accumulate. Adaptive
designs can include early stopping rules, sample size re-estimation, allocation ratio
changes, and patient enrichment strategies, all of which aim to extract the same
statistical information with fewer resources. Regulatory agencies, including the FDA
and EMA, have issued guidance documents supporting the use of adaptive designs when
they are scientifically rigorous and transparently pre-specified.

\section{Why Heterogeneous Treatment Effects Matter}

A fundamental limitation of the classical RCT paradigm is its focus on the Average
Treatment Effect (ATE) — the mean difference in outcomes between the treated and
control groups across the entire population. ATE is useful to understand the difference
between the treated and control groups, but it conceals a clinically critical dimension: not
every patient benefits equally from the same intervention. Two patients with opposite
bleeding and ischemic risk profiles, enrolled in the same trial and assigned to the same
treatment arm, may experience vastly different outcomes. A therapy that is beneficial
on average may be harmful for a specific subgroup, and vice versa.
The shift towards precision medicine demands methods that move beyond population-
average estimates and instead characterize how effects vary across individual patient
characteristics. This is precisely what the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE)
\[
\tau(x) = \mathbb{E}\left[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x\right]
\]
formalizes: the expected causal effect of treatment
relative to control, given that a patient has covariate profile $x$. Identifying patients
with large versus small (or even negative) CATE values is the goal of treatment effect
heterogeneity (HTE) analysis, which is increasingly recognized as essential for both
clinical practice and efficient trial design.

\section{The MASTER DAPT Trial as the Clinical Reference}

The \textit{MASTER DAPT} trial (\textit{Management of High Bleeding Risk
Patients Post Bioresorbable Polymer Coated Stent Implantation with an
Abbreviated versus Standard DAPT Regimen}; \citet{valgimigli2021masterdapt})
provides the primary clinical reference for this thesis. MASTER DAPT was a
multicenter, open-label, randomized noninferiority trial coordinated by the
European Cardiovascular Research Institute (ECRI) and funded by Terumo. A
total of 4,579 patients at high bleeding risk (HBR) were enrolled across
140 sites in 30 countries between February 2017 and December 2019. Patients
who had received a biodegradable-polymer sirolimus-eluting stent during
percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were randomized, one month after
the index procedure, to either an abbreviated Dual Antiplatelet Therapy
(DAPT) strategy (immediate discontinuation) or a standard DAPT strategy
(continuation for at least 5 additional months).

Three hierarchically ranked primary outcomes were assessed at 335 days: (1)
Net Adverse Clinical Events (NACE), a composite of death, myocardial
infarction, stroke, or major bleeding; (2) Major Adverse Cardiac or Cerebral
Events (MACCE), a composite of death, myocardial infarction, or stroke; and
(3) major or clinically relevant non-major (CRNM) bleeding. The trial
demonstrated noninferiority of the abbreviated strategy for NACE and MACCE,
and superiority with respect to bleeding reduction.

While MASTER DAPT established average-level conclusions, it enrolled a
clinically heterogeneous population in which the optimal DAPT duration
is likely to vary substantially across patients. Patients with primarily
ischemic risk profiles may benefit from longer protection, while those with
dominant bleeding risk may suffer more harm from prolonged therapy. This
clinical heterogeneity motivates reanalyzing and redesigning the trial through
a CATE-lens.

\section{Why Not Every Patient Benefits Equally}

The average-level conclusion of MASTER DAPT — that abbreviated therapy is
noninferior for ischemic events and superior for bleeding — masks a clinically
plausible and important hypothesis: that the optimal strategy differs across
patients. Consider two contrasting patient archetypes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A patient aged 85 with severe chronic kidney disease, active oral
    anticoagulation, and a recent gastrointestinal bleed: the dominant risk
    is bleeding, and abbreviated DAPT may be strongly beneficial.
  \item A 68-year-old diabetic patient who recently had a myocardial
    infarction, with a complex bifurcation stenting procedure: the dominant
    risk is ischemia, and prolonged DAPT may be preferable.
\end{itemize}

These archetypes illustrate treatment effect heterogeneity that cannot be
captured by ATE analysis but is the target of CATE estimation. Moreover, the
HBR population of MASTER DAPT is particularly heterogeneous: the mean number
of HBR criteria was $2.1 \pm 1.1$ per patient, spanning elderly frail patients,
patients on anticoagulation, and patients with multiple comorbidities.

\section{Aim and Scope of This Thesis}

This thesis reanalyses MASTER DAPT from a conditional treatment-effect
perspective. Its central question is whether the benefit--risk balance of
abbreviated versus standard DAPT varies systematically with patients' baseline
clinical characteristics and, if it does, whether that variation can support
individualized treatment decisions. The analysis estimates and validates CATEs
for the competing bleeding and ischemic outcomes, rather than seeking to
replace the trial's average-level conclusions.

The thesis then examines the practical implication of these estimates for trial
design. In particular, it asks whether enrolling patients with more informative
covariate profiles could make the detection and estimation of treatment-effect
heterogeneity more efficient. The following chapters introduce the CATE
estimation and validation framework, formulate and evaluate the guided
enrolment strategy, describe the MASTER DAPT data and clinical endpoints, and
discuss the resulting implications and limitations.

\paragraph{Thesis structure.}
The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:01} introduces the clinical and statistical
    background, motivating the need for conditional treatment-effect
    estimation and guided enrollment.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:02} presents the methods used to estimate and
    validate CATEs, including the statistical framework, machine learning
    models, and evaluation metrics.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:03} examines how these estimates can be translated
    into patient-ranking and enrollment strategies, together with the criteria
    used to evaluate them.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:04} describes the MASTER DAPT dataset, the treatment
    comparison, and the clinical endpoints considered in the analysis.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:05} investigates the predictive structure of these
    endpoints, providing the basis for the subsequent heterogeneity analysis.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:06} presents the estimated treatment-effect
    heterogeneity and introduces the trade-off framework used to combine
    bleeding and ischemic outcomes.
  \item Chapter~\ref{chap:07} applies the resulting ranking strategies to
    simulated guided enrollment in MASTER DAPT, assessing whether the estimated
    heterogeneity can be exploited in practice.
  \item Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:08} discusses the main findings,
    limitations, and implications for future research.
\end{itemize}

\section{Chapter Summary}
This chapter contains the introduction to the thesis, motivating the need 
for conditional treatment-effect estimation and guided enrollment. It presents the
MASTER DAPT trial as the starting point for the analysis and explains the 
metric of interest, the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), which captures
the heterogeneity of treatment effects across patients. The chapter also outlines the
aim and scope of the thesis, emphasizing the importance of understanding how treatment
effects vary with patient characteristics and how this knowledge can inform more efficient trial designs.
