\section{Five Estimators on the Cohort}

This chapter applies the machinery introduced in Chapter~2 to estimate
treatment-effect heterogeneity in the MASTER DAPT cohort. The main point is
answering four questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Is there evidence of treatment-effect heterogeneity across patients?
  \item If so, does the treatment benefit some patients more than others?
  \item Can we derive decisions ranking the patients by their predicted benefit, 
  exploiting the estimated heterogeneity with an online learning approach?
  \item How large would have the hetorogeneity needed to be in order to be detected by the five eistimators of Chapter~2 (\S~\ref{sec:five-estimators})
        (\S~\ref{sec:delta})?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implementation on this cohort.}
The five estimator families of Chapter~2 (\S~\ref{sec:five-estimators}) are
fitted on the same 4,579 patients, the same covariate set and the same five
endpoints of Chapter 4: the two-model meta-learner, using the calibrated
per-arm random forests of Section~\ref{sec:per-arm}; a causal forest
(\texttt{CausalForestDML}) with gradient-boosted first stages for both
nuisance functions $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid x]$ and $\mathbb{E}[T \mid x]$; a
Bayesian causal forest (BCF) with the propensity fixed at the known $0.5$;
an in-context model (CausalPFN); and an interaction forest (S-learner). As
established in Chapter~2, their failure modes do not overlap, so a result
common to all five is hard to attribute to any single one of them.

\paragraph{The common protocol.}
All estimators use the same preprocessing pipeline, with k-NN imputation and
robust scaling fitted only on the training data. Heterogeneity is evaluated
by fitting the model on 70\% of the data, ranking the held-out 30\% by
predicted CATE, and applying the doubly robust tester of Chapter~2
(\S~\ref{sec:validation}) with the known randomization propensity, reporting
the BLP slope, AUTOC, and QINI defined there.

Consequently, the S-learner's dispersion can be viewed as an \emph{upper
bound} on genuine heterogeneity: if even this bound is uninformative, the
evidence for meaningful heterogeneity is even weaker.

\section{The Negative Result: The Dispersion Is Noise}

Every estimator produces a distribution of per-patient effects, and every such
distribution has non-zero width. That width, on its own, means nothing: a
constant true effect estimated with error also produces a spread of estimates,
and the width of that spread grows with the flexibility of the model, not with
the amount of heterogeneity in the data. The question is therefore never
whether the dispersion is positive, but whether it exceeds the noise floor, and whether the resulting \emph{ranking} of patients tracks
anything real.

\paragraph{Dispersion, and a noise floor to compare it against.}
Table~\ref{tab:cate-dispersion} reports the standard deviation of the
per-patient CATE for each estimator. The five columns disagree by an order of
magnitude — from $0.003$ to $0.093$ on the same patients and the same endpoint
— which is itself the first indication that what is being measured is a
property of the estimators rather than of the cohort. If the spread reflected
real variation in treatment response, it would not depend so strongly on who
is measuring it.

The last column is what turns the comparison into a test. Permuting the
treatment labels destroys the effect while leaving the covariates, the outcome
rates and the estimation procedure untouched, so refitting on permuted data
measures exactly how much apparent heterogeneity the method manufactures from
nothing. On bleeding, the BCF's observed spread is $0.0390$ against a placebo
floor of $0.0309$, an excess of $0.0081$. On BARC 2/3/5, the observed spread is
$0.0276$ against a floor of $0.0264$, an excess of $0.0012$. These values come
from the latest run of 100 treatment-label permutations, with 500 retained
posterior draws per fit, and correspond to the rounded values reported in
Table~\ref{tab:cate-dispersion}.

As a descriptive diagnostic, the fraction of posterior draws from the observed
fit that exceed the pooled placebo mean is calculated as
\[
D
=
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\mathbb{I}\!\left(s_m^{\mathrm{obs}}>
\overline{s}^{\mathrm{placebo}}\right),
\qquad
s_m = \mathrm{sd}_i\!\left(\tau_m(X_i)\right),
\]
where $M=500$ is the number of retained posterior draws from the observed fit.
The resulting values are $0.824$ for bleeding and $0.548$ for BARC 2/3/5. This
quantity compares the observed posterior with the mean of the pooled placebo
distribution; it does not propagate uncertainty in that placebo distribution
and should therefore not be interpreted as a formal posterior probability or
a hypothesis-test $p$-value. The result suggests a modest separation from the
noise floor for bleeding and essentially no separation for BARC 2/3/5, rather
than strong evidence of treatment-effect heterogeneity. Figure~\ref{fig:placebo-floor}
shows the corresponding distributions.

The BLP slope (\S~\ref{sec:validation}) provides a similarly weak indication
of treatment-effect heterogeneity.
\vspace{10pt}

For all endpoints except stroke, the regression of the
doubly robust effect score on the predicted CATE yields a negative slope,
including a slope of $-3.16$ for bleeding ($p = 0.25$). Thus, the estimated
treatment-effect ranking does not show a positive association with the
observed effect scores; the estimated association is instead slightly
negative, although it is not statistically distinguishable from zero.
Calibration is likewise poor: the calibration $R^2$ is negative for three
of the five endpoints and close to zero for a fourth. This indicates that,
for these endpoints, the model-based group-level treatment-effect
predictions do not explain more variation in the observed group effects
than the corresponding overall mean.

\vspace{10pt}

The two remaining estimators are evaluated with weaker but independent
protocols and reach the same place. The in-context model's bootstrapped TOC
bands cover zero on every endpoint, and its strongest Spearman correlation
between any covariate and the predicted CATE is $0.21$ — no patient
characteristic meaningfully moves its estimate. 
The interaction forest points
the same way from inside its own design matrix: the treatment column carries
between $0.0009$ and $0.0018$ of the forest's total splitting importance
depending on the endpoint, so the ensemble that is free to split on treatment,
on covariates and on their product barely uses the treatment at all.

the two sides that matter. The sorted CATE curves are monotone by construction
and therefore always look like heterogeneity; what they actually show, on the
bleeding endpoints, is a cohort in which every single patient is estimated to
benefit from abbreviating, spanning $+0.032$ to $+0.068$ around a mean of
$+0.047$ — a total range smaller than the average effect itself. The TOC curves then ask whether that ordering buys anything,
and answer no: at every treated fraction, the doubly-robust gain over random
targeting is indistinguishable from zero.

\paragraph{What the two results say together.}
Read separately, either half could be dismissed. A positive control on its own
shows that the estimators can find an average effect, which is a much easier
task than finding heterogeneity. A null result on its own is compatible with
having searched badly. Together they are informative: the same estimators, the
same data, the same validation protocol recover an average effect that matches
the published trial and fail to find any variation around it above the level
that noise alone produces. The distinction this chapter is entitled to claim is
between \emph{nothing was found} and \emph{a search with tools that demonstrably
work found nothing} --- and Section~\ref{sec:delta} makes even that claim
quantitative by measuring how large a heterogeneity those tools would have
needed in order to find it.

\section{An Episode: The Model That Collapsed}
\label{sec:tuned-forest}

Following Chapter 2, the causal forest's hyperparameters are tuned on the same
criterion used to evaluate it: the cross-validated lower confidence bound of
the AUTOC on the bleeding endpoint, 
\[
\mathrm{AUTOC} - z \cdot \mathrm{SE}
\]
averaged over folds. The search is deliberately wide and it is run as a parallel 
Optuna study reporting the results in a shared journal;
the configuration reported here was selected over 23,361 completed trials. 
Two methods are used to avoid the winner's curse. First the performance of the model
is evaluated against the lower bound of the AUTOC calculated with the formula cited above.
This penalizes configurations whose apparently strong ranking performance is accompanied
by high uncertainty, reducing the chance of selecting a model simply because of a
favourable but unstable cross-validation estimate. Secondly if the model returns a
constant cate across the patients, the AUTOC is set to zero, so that 
the model cannot win by returning a degenerate ranking.

That second safeguard is motivated by an earlier diagnostic result. Under one
tuned configuration, $\mathrm{std}(\mathrm{CATE})$ was exactly zero, meaning
that the estimated treatment effect was identical for all patients. The cause
was traced to the forest's \texttt{min\_impurity\_decrease} hyperparameter:
when set too high, no covariate provides sufficient improvement to justify a
split, and the forest therefore returns the same effect estimate for every
patient, corresponding to the overall ATE. This is not a pipeline error, but
a valid limiting case in which the data provide insufficient evidence of
treatment-effect heterogeneity under the chosen splitting criterion.

\paragraph{The flexibility ladder.}
Rather than settle the question with a single tuned model, three explicit
configurations of increasing capacity are fitted, scaling the forest and its
first-stage nuisance models together: \texttt{regularized} (depth 2, leaves of
at least 40), \texttt{medium} (depth 4, leaves of 15) and \texttt{flexible}
(depth 8, leaves of 5). Table~\ref{tab:flex-ladder} reports the results.

Even varying the model configurations from the most to the least regularized, 
the mean CATE does not variate at all: bleeding stays between 0.045 and 0.047 across all configurations,
while the dispersion grows by a factor of four.
Under the regularized preset the three ischemic endpoints collapse to a constant: stroke
takes exactly one distinct value across all 4,579 patients, and cardiovascular
death and MI take three, spanning a range of $2 \times 10^{-5}$ and
$1 \times 10^{-4}$ respectively. Under the flexible preset the same endpoints
show dispersions of $0.011$ and $0.012$, comfortably larger than their own
average effects.

A quantity that is invariant to model capacity is a property of the data; a
quantity that scales with model capacity is a property of the model.
This results clearly illustrate that the averge treatment effect is 
property of the data, while the heterogeneity is property of the model.
We have to understand that the heterogeneity could be just product of the
noise present in the data and the models are just overfitting to the noise.

\paragraph{A note on the tuning score itself.}
The best cross-validated AUTOC lower bound found by the search was $+0.014$ on
the bleeding endpoint, positive and encouraging in isolation. The same model,
evaluated once on a held-out split by the doubly-robust tester, returns
$-0.013$ ($p = 0.18$). The maximum of a noisy criterion over tens of thousands
of configurations is itself an optimistic statistic, and the lower-bound
objective attenuates that optimism without eliminating it. For this reason no
heterogeneity claim in this thesis rests on a tuning score: the held-out
doubly-robust evaluation is the only figure of merit used to support the
conclusions.

The Optuna study continued running after this configuration was selected, and
has since reached 161,336 trials and a new best cross-validated AUTOC lower
bound of $+0.017$. That later configuration has never been evaluated on a
held-out split, so it is not used anywhere in this chapter: a higher tuning
score with no held-out check is exactly the optimistic statistic the paragraph
above warns against, not a stronger result.

\section{The Trade-off Plane}
The results above are endpoint specific, whereas the clinical decision
requires balancing bleeding and ischemic outcomes jointly. The endpoint-
specific CATEs can therefore be represented in a two-dimensional trade-off
plane, with the bleeding benefit of abbreviated therapy on one axis and the
ischemic effect on the other. The top-right quadrant favours abbreviated
therapy, while the bottom-left favours prolonged therapy. The bottom-right
quadrant is the most relevant for personalization, as abbreviated therapy
reduces bleeding but increases ischemic risk. The ischemic axis combines MI,
stroke, and cardiovascular death using the prespecified weights $0.4$, $0.4$,
and $0.2$, respectively. Both axes are read from the single tuned causal
forest of Section~\ref{sec:tuned-forest}, the same model used for the
sorted-CATE and TOC figures earlier in this chapter, rather than from all
five estimators of Section~\ref{sec:positive-control}: the plane is a
two-dimensional view of one model's ranking, not a cross-estimator
comparison.

\paragraph{The plane with uncertainty.}
A single fitted plane does not quantify uncertainty in individual treatment
effects. We therefore use a nonparametric bootstrap, resampling patients with
replacement, refitting the tuned forest, and predicting on the original
covariates. Fifty replicates provide a 95\% interval for each patient's
position on both axes. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff-bootstrap}, none of the $4{,}579$ patients
has its entire confidence region within the bottom-right quadrant, despite
$1{,}544$ point estimates falling there. The within-patient bootstrap
variation is also large relative to the variation across patients, with
signal-to-noise ratios of $0.57$ for bleeding and $0.52$ for ischemic risk,
indicating that individual differences in the estimated treatment effects are
largely dominated by estimation uncertainty. In contrast, $4{,}541$ patients
($99.2\%$) have a significantly positive bleeding benefit from abbreviated
therapy. Thus, the analysis supports a strong average bleeding benefit, but
provides little evidence of reliably distinguishable patient-level
heterogeneity.

\paragraph{A caution about the conflict subgroup.}
When we sort by CAE the training test and we use the top 15\% by conflict 
score produces a subgroup of 687 patients
whose \emph{observed} event rates are very high: 24.6\% bleeding under
prolonged therapy against 3.7\% under abbreviated, a raw difference of 20.8
percentage points, against 1.6 points in the rest of the cohort. The number is
real and it means nothing, because the score that selected those patients was
computed from a model fitted on the same patients and the same outcomes. The
subgroup is selected \emph{to} exhibit that contrast; quoting its observed
event rates as an effect estimate would be circular. It is reported here as a
descriptive, in-sample profile only, and the honest counterpart is the previous
paragraph: with error bars, none of these patients is distinguishable from the
cohort average.

\section{From Ranking to Decision}
\label{sec:decision}

The analyses above assess whether the model's patient ranking captures
meaningful differences in treatment effect. A distinct and clinically more
relevant question is whether acting on this ranking yields a measurable
benefit. A decision rule may provide value even when the underlying ranking
is imperfect, while a well-calibrated ranking may have limited practical
utility if the benefit it identifies is too small to justify departing from
a simple treatment strategy. These questions therefore require different
analytical approaches, and the negative findings above do not, by
themselves, determine the value of the resulting treatment policy.

\paragraph{The net-benefit endpoint.}
A decision needs an outcome that already encodes the trade-off, since no single
endpoint can. This section therefore adds two endpoints already adjudicated by
the trial's Clinical Event Committee rather than built for this thesis: NACE
(Net Adverse Clinical Events, 7.7\% in this cohort), the trial's pre-specified
primary net-benefit endpoint, combining death, MI, stroke and BARC 3--5
bleeding, and the ischemic composite MACCE (death, MI and stroke, 6.0\%)
\citep{valgimigli2021masterdapt}. Both
are read directly from the merged MASTER DAPT dataset, as the columns
\texttt{cec\_nace\_335d} and \texttt{cec\_macce\_335d}, and neither passes
through the five-endpoint modelling pipeline of Chapter 4 or the CATE
estimators earlier in this chapter: they enter the analysis only here, with
their own outcome and propensity models fitted fresh under cross-fitted AIPW,
independently of every result so far. All estimates below use the known
randomization propensity.

The analysis is first anchored to the trial-level effects. Table~6.5 reports
the doubly robust ATE for NACE and MACCE, together with the corresponding
component effects, using this common AIPW pipeline. The NACE confidence
interval includes zero, indicating no detectable overall treatment effect and
reproducing the trial's primary finding. At the component level, abbreviated
therapy shows a bleeding benefit, while the ischemic effect is not
statistically distinguishable from zero. The two effects therefore largely
offset each other at the composite level.

\paragraph{Policy value.}
A treatment policy $\pi$ maps a patient's covariates to a recommended arm, abbreviated or prolonged DAPT. This section compares five such policies on NACE, under the same cross-fitted AIPW framework as above.

Two are the fixed arms. A third, \emph{ATE sign}, is a data-driven constant rule: on each training fold it recommends whichever arm that fold's estimated ATE favors, so across the five folds its overall percentage of patients recommended prolonged therapy need not be 0 or 100. The remaining two learn a covariate-dependent recommendation directly, by minimizing the expected event rate: an interpretable depth-2 doubly-robust policy tree and a doubly-robust policy forest (200 trees, depth 3), both fitted with \texttt{econml}'s \texttt{DRPolicyTree} and \texttt{DRPolicyForest} under the known randomization propensity.

Evaluation follows the same five-fold discipline as the ATE estimates: each learned policy is fitted on the training folds and scored only on the held-out fold, so no policy is evaluated on patients it was trained on. A policy's \emph{value} is its out-of-fold expected NACE rate (lower is better); its \emph{improvement} is the paired, per-patient difference against whichever fixed arm scores lower, with a confidence interval on that paired difference (Table~\ref{tab:policy-value}).

heterogeneity that is not present in the data: every patient a rule diverts to
prolonged therapy is, on average, a patient who gains nothing and bleeds
more.

\section{How Small a Heterogeneity Would Have Been Seen}
\label{sec:delta}

The positive control in Section~6.2 establishes that the estimators recover an
average treatment effect, but this does not demonstrate that they are capable
of detecting treatment-effect heterogeneity of a given magnitude. These are
distinct properties, and the latter is essential when interpreting a negative
heterogeneity result. Without such an assessment, the conclusion that no
heterogeneity was detected remains a statement about the analysis rather than
about the underlying data. This section therefore quantifies the sensitivity
of the analysis by estimating, for each endpoint, the smallest heterogeneity
$\delta$ that the protocol used in this chapter could reliably detect.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
The cohort's real covariates and real treatment assignment are left untouched;
only the outcome is resampled. A baseline risk $\mu(x)$ is fitted on the
abbreviated arm, a direction of heterogeneity $g(x)$ is fixed as the
standardized patient age — chosen because it is the second most important
covariate in the already-fitted bleeding model, and because age is a recognised
component of standard bleeding-risk scores, so the injected modifier is
clinically plausible rather than arbitrary — and synthetic outcomes are drawn
from the following equation.

\begin{equation}
Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(
\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mu(x) + T \cdot \delta \cdot g(x)\right)
\right).
\end{equation}

For the two bleeding endpoints $\delta$ is an absolute risk
difference; for the three rare endpoints an additive $\delta$ of comparable
size would distort the prevalence, so it is applied as a log odds ratio on the
logit scale. The tuned causal forest is then refitted with unchanged
hyperparameters and scored with the same cross-validated AUTOC lower bound used
everywhere else.

The comparison at each $\delta$ is not against zero but against a noise floor
recomputed \emph{at that same} $\delta$ by permuting the treatment: the same
synthetic outcomes, the same estimator, no effect. This matters because the
floor is not constant — a rarer or more skewed outcome shifts it — and
comparing against a fixed zero would credit the protocol with detections it did
not make. With 20 seeds per $(\text{endpoint}, \delta)$ pair and 500 fits in
total, $\delta^*$ is defined as the smallest $\delta$ on the grid at which the
observed score beats its paired floor in at least 90\% of seeds.

\centering

\midrule

\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Reading the number honestly.}
The current sensitivity results are interpretable only for the two bleeding endpoints.
For bleeding, the minimum detectable risk difference is 0.100, more than twice
the observed average effect of 0.045. For BARC 2/3/5 bleeding it is 0.050,
larger than the observed average effect of 0.028. These results exclude only
large heterogeneity; they do not establish that smaller patient-level effects
are absent.

The ischemic rows fail the null-calibration check. At zero injected
heterogeneity, MI and cardiovascular death already meet the nominal detection
criterion, while stroke reaches 80 percent of seeds without reaching the
required 90 percent. These artifacts do not demonstrate sensitivity to small
heterogeneity and the associated thresholds must not be interpreted as valid
power estimates.

\section{Chapter Summary}

Four conclusions emerge from the analyses, in the order in which they were
established. First, the average treatment effect in MASTER DAPT is recovered
consistently across five independent estimator families and agrees with the
published trial results, supporting the validity of the estimation framework.
Second, the estimated patient-level variation does not exceed the heterogeneity
observed under treatment-label permutation, does not persist in doubly robust
held-out evaluation under either weighting scheme, and increases with model
capacity in a pattern consistent with estimation artefacts rather than a
stable property of the data. Third, the trade-off plane that would underpin a
personalized treatment rule forms a single compact distribution, with no
patient whose position in the clinically relevant quadrant remains supported
by the bootstrap. Finally, direct evaluation of treatment policies through
cross-fitted policy values leads to the same conclusion: no personalized
policy improves upon abbreviated therapy across the considered weighting of
the bleeding--ischemic trade-off.

The sensitivity analysis places these findings in context. For the bleeding
endpoints, the protocol would have detected heterogeneity comparable to the
trial's average treatment effect, yet no such heterogeneity is observed. For
the ischemic endpoints, reliable detection would require substantially larger
effect modification, making the question difficult to resolve with this
cohort alone.

MASTER DAPT enrolls only patients already flagged as high bleeding risk
(ARC-HBR criterion, \citealp{valgimigli2021appendix}), so predicted bleeding risk varies little across the cohort,
leaving the causal forest little covariate-space variation to exploit 
regardless of the 506 observed events.

On the ischemic side, MACCE (death, MI, stroke) shows a null ATE in Table 6.5 
($-0.000$, CI $[-0.014,+0.013]$) while the bleeding component is significant 
($+0.027$): a null composite is consistent with a genuine absence of effect on 
each component, but equally consistent with opposite-direction effects (e.g. MI and stroke) 
cancelling in the sum. Together, the two facts explain a strong average effect 
alongside near-zero estimated heterogeneity: trial selection compresses the risk 
variation on the bleeding side, aggregation masks it on the ischemic side.
